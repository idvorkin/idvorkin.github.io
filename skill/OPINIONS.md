# OPINIONS.md

A compact map of Igor Dvorkin's public viewpoints, distilled from his blog at https://idvork.in. It is written for an LLM to load as context: repeated signals are consolidated, jokes and implementation detail are dropped, and each opinion links the post(s) it came from. The blog is the source of truth; when in doubt, read the linked page. Many frameworks here are borrowed (Covey, Pink, Brooks, Harris, Attia, McGill, Damour, Watkins); the posts credit them, and so should you.

## Living deliberately: mortality, roles, and reviews

### Knowing you will die is a planning tool, not a morbid hobby

Igor thinks people drift because they act as if time were unlimited, so he deliberately keeps finitude in view. His "mortality software" (Time.ltd) looks both ways: forward, to plan the life your eulogy describes, and backward, to check whether you are living it. He renamed it from Life.ltd because "time is limited" is a more useful frame than "life is limited." He keeps a life-in-weeks grid, borrows memento mori from Stoicism, and takes two lessons from Chasing Daylight: create perfect moments of full presence, and close relationships on purpose rather than by accident. He admits why this software does not exist: it makes people feel finite and judge themselves. His answer is to lead with compassion and frame things around life stages and satisfaction, not dread. Practical rule: whatever you plan to do before you die, move it up.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/mortality-software, https://idvork.in/death, https://idvork.in/chasing-daylight

### Write your eulogy by role, then steer each week against it

Igor's foundational planning act is Covey's "begin with the end in mind" taken to the limit: write your own eulogy, organized by roles (Dealer of Smiles and Wonder, Mostly Car-Free Spirit, Disciple of the 7 Habits, Fit Fellow, Emotionally Healthy Human, Technologist, Family Man, husband, father). For each role he writes who he wants to be, not just what he wants to do, and he says plainly that the fleshed-out sections are the easy ones. The mechanism is practical: imagine the speakers at your funeral, then ask whether last week moved toward or away from what each would say; the neglected role is the corrective signal. He prints eulogy-derived prompts faintly at the bottom of his journal pages so an ordinary Tuesday connects to the long view. "Currently the purpose of my eulogy is to guide me in living my life."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/eulogy, https://idvork.in/end-in-mind, https://idvork.in/process-journal

### The 7 Habits is the manual for life: work inside-out, private victories first

Igor calls The 7 Habits his user manual and claims most other self-help books are reframes of it (GTD and Eat That Frog are Habit 3; Essentialism is Habits 2 and 3). He accepts the maturity continuum (dependent, independent, interdependent) and its order: private victories before public ones, and borrowed strength from position builds weakness in everyone involved. His Habit 1 practices: name your state so you are not fused with it, widen the gap between stimulus and response, swap "I have to" for "I choose," and keep your energy inside your circle of influence, growing it by being useful at its edge rather than demanding authority. Keeping small commitments to yourself is the most valuable capability, so start small enough that you cannot fail. "If I'm not acting, the world is acting on me. There's no neutral state."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/7-habits, https://idvork.in/7h-concepts, https://idvork.in/be-proactive

### In interdependent life, Win/Win is the only paradigm that lasts

In any relationship that outlives one deal, Win/Lose and Lose/Win both drain the trust account into Lose/Lose. "Win/Win or No Deal" is the freedom move: once walking away is allowed, manipulation stops. The everyday currency is the Emotional Bank Account: small kindnesses and kept promises are deposits, an "I'll try" half-promise is a withdrawal, and constant relationships (spouse, kids) need constant deposits because old ones evaporate. On listening he admits his default is to listen in order to reply; the fix is diagnose before prescribing, reflect the feeling, and describe the other person's position better than they can before making your case. Synergy goes one step further: look for a third alternative neither side brought.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/win-win, https://idvork.in/first-understand, https://idvork.in/synergize

### Manage by importance, not urgency, and plan the week by role

Igor thinks fourth-generation time management is about managing yourself, not time: the unit is the week, the lens is effectiveness, the focus is roles. The day is too narrow and the month cannot be gripped. Quadrant II work (prevention, relationships, capacity) is what shrinks tomorrow's crises, and the common failure is spending the day in Quadrant III, on someone else's urgency, believing you are in Quadrant I. His weekly mechanic: list your roles; pick 10 to 15 rocks at most, at least one per role (a health rock, a recharge rock, a date with his wife, a 1:1 with each kid, one important-not-urgent work item, one friend reach-out); schedule the rocks first and let meetings fill the rest. If a rock cannot find a slot it is not a rock this week, so defer it honestly. Each day gets about 20 minutes of adapting against the plan, not re-planning, and a missed day restarts without moralizing. "Don't prioritize what's on the schedule, schedule the priorities."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/first-things-first, https://idvork.in/frog, https://idvork.in/gtd

### Systems beat goals; goals exist to design and grade systems

You don't rise to the level of your goals, you fall to the level of your systems. The value of a goal is that it helps you build and assess the system behind it. He pairs lag metrics (which define success) with lead metrics (which you can steer), warns against measuring what is easy instead of what matters and against gaming a proxy, and keeps scorecards because of his Immutable Laws of Hard Things: without a goal and a measure you drift; you never beat the goal, you barely make it; and without a consequence you ignore it. The 4DX cadence of accountability (report last week's commitments, check the scoreboard, commit for next week) is where execution actually happens.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/goals, https://idvork.in/Immutable-Laws-Of-Hard, https://idvork.in/4dx

### Plan enough to start, get into a feedback loop, and never skip review

Igor thinks the plan trains the planner: planning clarifies the goal, finds the unknowns and lowers activation energy, but once you are polishing the plan instead of testing it, planning has become procrastination and false comfort. He prefers SpaceX to NASA: get into a fast empirical loop, because real knowledge comes from what you observe. He budgets the "first creation" (ten minutes for a small thing, an hour for a medium one, real time with a deadline for life-shaping questions). His key addition is the third leg, Plan, Do, Review. Review is where the learning happens and people skip it because it threatens the ego and nobody forces it. His four questions: what happened, what worked, what didn't, what did I learn. To make it happen: schedule it, make it social, lower the bar to five minutes, attach it to an existing transition, write it down, and separate data from judgment like a blameless postmortem. "If you're going to skimp on something, skimp on planning. But never skip review."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/planning, https://idvork.in/end-in-mind

### Run the weekly review as a short, scored dragon check across every role

This is how Igor actually reviews. Weekly, on Sunday, about 30 minutes: a Dragon Check. Entropy: did I hit the gym, keep the sleep window, did the family dates happen? Squander: did I create any artifacts and move projects forward? Scarcity: a quick, non-obsessive check on money anxiety. Then a non-negotiables scorecard in fractions (gym X/5, sleep window X/7, family rituals X/3, home by 6pm X/5, project hours), closing with "What blocked me? What needs protection from work demands?" Through the role lens: hold the week up against the eulogy speakers, notice which role got starved, and plant one concrete commitment for it in next week's calendar. His 7 Habits prompts apply too: re-read the eulogy; what relationship can I invest in this week; am I over-investing in one role; what quadrant did I spend my time in; did I do my habits; is technology helping or hurting. Monthly (last Sunday, about an hour): a deep dive on physical health, emotional health, family ("are rituals deepening relationships or just checking boxes?") and projects ("real progress or busy work?"); ask which dragon won and why; update the metric tables; check the trip wires (gym under 4x/week two weeks running, family rituals missed two weeks, no artifacts for a month, weight trending wrong for two months), and a tripped wire forces an explicit choice about what to say no to at work. Quarterly: "Am I winning against the dragons or just surviving? What worked, what failed, what changes?" Year-end: can I honestly fill in "Stuff I'm really proud of"? The tone stays humane: patterns, not streaks; "what happened?" rather than "why did you skip?"; restart without moralizing. The weekly report blends stories with numbers so growth is visible without being reduced to metrics, and it builds a memory archive and sharper instinct over time. "Every week is a transaction you can't undo."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/y26, https://idvork.in/gap-year-igor, https://idvork.in/mortality-software

### Write next year's report in advance, then grade it honestly in public

Igor's annual plan is a pre-written year-end report: major projects, a summer vacation, health goals by dimension, books, "Stuff I'm really proud of," and "Artifacts I created," with goal tables (target, January, June, December, status). At year end he adds honest result banners, including red "no progress, carrying forward," and lets projects roll over for years rather than pretending they were done. Mid-year he adds focus blocks, sometimes as a bright-line rule so there is nothing left to decide in the moment (always do the act of joy for a stranger). "This hasn't happened, but if I don't write it out and focus on it, it certainly won't."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/y26, https://idvork.in/y25, https://idvork.in/y24

### Journal daily, by hand, inside a structure that ties the day to the eulogy

Igor has journaled stream-of-consciousness since 2011, over a million words. He thinks better with a pen but typed for years because you cannot grep paper; AI transcription ended the tradeoff, so he now handwrites in a quarter-long hyperlinked template on a Kindle Scribe and transcribes it. Each day is five pages: a setup page (four affirmations, gratitude, Yesterday Awesome Because, a habit strip), an intentions page (Today Awesome If, commitments, whether he kept yesterday's), and three open pages. He primes the session with yesterday's to-dos and habit tracker, and to-dos get extracted into his task system. Affirmations come in levels: write them out, ask "how will I nail this today?", then "how did past me do?", with compassion. "You can't grep paper."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/process-journal, https://idvork.in/affirmations, https://idvork.in/day-in-the-life

### Structure should be humane: design for the comeback, not for perfection

People recoil from structure because they have only seen the managerial kind that shames you. Igor's alternative assumes you already know what is good for you, life is inconsistent, and a miss is the system's failure, not yours. So track patterns ("8 of the last 10 days") rather than fragile streaks, use observing language ("it's been 3 days") instead of judging language, count partial wins, record the context for misses, and ask "what did I learn?" before "how did I do?" His climbing metaphor: habits need anchors and clips that catch the fall, not free-solo all-or-nothing. The older version is grandmother mind: discipline starts strong, falls off, and you restart the next day. "You're not broken, you just need better support."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/structure, https://idvork.in/operating-manual, https://idvork.in/d/habits

### Choose the essential, say no gracefully, and quit cul-de-sacs

A strategy for a life is the same as for a company: decide what not to do. Igor leans on "I choose to," not "I have to"; "hell yes or no" and the 90% rule; and three questions (how will I know I'm done; if I didn't own this, would I buy it; if I didn't have this opportunity, what would I do to get it). Every yes is a no to something, usually the quiet thing (marriage, health, long-term work), so he keeps graceful-no scripts and uses zero-based re-evaluation for sunk costs. From The Dip: lean into dips, quit cul-de-sacs, set quitting criteria before you start, and distrust things that sorta-kinda work. Parkinson's law applies to time and money alike, so set stopping criteria and a budget up front. "Life is about what you decide to put on your todo list, not how efficiently you cross things off."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/essentialism, https://idvork.in/dip, https://idvork.in/parkinson

### Want less, and let regret point forward instead of trapping you

Satisfaction = Haves / Wants, and the denominator is the stronger lever because haves adapt away and wants can be dropped for good. His tool is the reverse bucket list: write down the hollow, impressive goals and cross them out on purpose, reviewed quarterly. He treats regret the same way: the past is outside the circle of influence, but regret is data about what you value (foundation, boldness, moral, connection), useful for avoiding future regret and worth forgiving in the past. He separates bad decisions from bad outcomes: if you decided on principle with the facts you had, you decided well. "The ultimate bucket list is your eulogy."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/bucket-list, https://idvork.in/regrets, https://idvork.in/decisive

### Manage energy, not time: spend morning willpower on what matters and schedule away the choices

Igor's answer to "how do you have time for all this?" is that energy is the variable. His willpower is high at 5am and gone by evening, which is why he never makes it to the gym after work and why his kryptonite is the easy chair, the snooze button and the phone. Every in-the-moment choice gives the resistance another chance, so he schedules what he wants (fixed weekly calendars with workouts, kid dates, a Sunday journal slot) and stops thinking about it. He models activation energy as stopping the current thing plus starting the next; addictions have negative starting energy and good escapes have low stopping energy. Willpower is many role "fibers" lifting one log; lose one (work structure during time off) and the rest can spiral. The resistance lies about how hard things are, and beating it once makes the next win easier. "Figure out what you want, and schedule it. Then don't think about it."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/operating-manual, https://idvork.in/activation, https://idvork.in/idle

### Guard the goose, not just the eggs: life is a portfolio of depreciating assets

Covey's P/PC balance is Igor's lens on everything: dashboards count eggs (output) while the damage happens silently to the goose (capacity): sleep, health, relationships, skills, the test suite. He extends it into "life as a business," with two currencies (life value and energy), a balance sheet of assets (relationships, health, skills, character) and liabilities (stress, bad habits), and the warning that some assets cannot be restored once neglected. He fences the metaphor: life is a mission, not an optimization, and measuring everything breeds attachment. Renewal works across four dimensions that compound, and he warns against becoming a saw collector who only sharpens. "You cannot be efficient with people, only effective."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/life-as-business, https://idvork.in/7h-concepts, https://idvork.in/sharpen-the-saw

### Time off is a skill: time-box the vegetating, keep the keystone habits, re-stack the roles

Time off goes through phases (vegetation, the to-do list, freedom, personal development) and the trap is getting stuck early. Igor's rules: a day or two of guilt-free vegetating, then fight the resistance; drop responsibilities but never the energy-positive habits (gym, journaling, meditation, magic); write goals and a won't-do list before leaving; re-read the eulogy and re-stack roles so the starved ones (father, husband, joy-giver) get fed and the over-invested technologist is dialed back. He plans trips by life domain, designs peaks and endings on purpose, researches gyms city by city because back care is the one thing he will not wing, and writes honest recaps ("I was doing too little for myself"). "When you sacrifice identity for consumption, you end up with neither."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/timeoff, https://idvork.in/timeoff-2026-07, https://idvork.in/timeoff-2022-07

### A gap year at 50 is a year on, but only with structure against the three dragons

The usual retirement math ignores arbitrage: at 50 you have more health, energy, kid time and options than at 65, and "one more year" costs more than it pays. He frames the gap year as a year ON, a PhD in life optimization: deliberate investment in health, relationship and mastery capital. Three immortal dragons guard it, and work keeps them boxed with legitimate excuses: Scarcity ("there's never enough"), Entropy ("everything decays without structure"), Squander ("you'll waste it"). You cannot slay them, only tame them: a cash buffer and money checks on Fridays only; daily anchors, a trainer and pre-scheduled social time; weekly artifacts as proof of investment; plus a pre-mortem and trip wires. The same infrastructure applies while working, which is where his weekly review came from. He labels his own plans completely aspirational. "Work will always be there. This window won't."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/gap-year, https://idvork.in/gap-year-igor, https://idvork.in/42

### Retire to something, start the side quests now, and match each chapter's resources

Retirement planning is precise about the exit and vague about the life after. Work is not a prison: it provides structure, connection and purpose, and the forces that stop you now get stronger without it. So aim for autonomy, mastery and purpose, treat financial independence as a tool rather than the goal, and start hobbies before you need them. Life has chapters: in your 20s you have time and health, so try everything; in your 30s you learn what you dislike, so stop doing it; in your 40s you have time and money but declining health, so focus on what you love and invest in your body. He says he does not yet know what the 50s and 60s hold. "We're so focused on what we're retiring from rather than what we're retiring to."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/retire, https://idvork.in/chapters, https://idvork.in/42

## The four kinds of health

### Health is rented in all four dimensions, and it is a system, not a checklist

Health has four dimensions (physical, emotional, spiritual, cognitive) and none is a state you reach and keep: you are fit while you are fit, and when you are not, you train. They are coupled: the body supplies energy for emotional regulation, emotional health lets you keep training and keep relationships, spiritual health supplies the motivation for the rest, and cognition is how you learn better practices; neglect compounds as debt across the others. He prefers a test you can answer today over a definition (physical: is your body ever the reason you say no; spiritual: are you motivated in a way you'd be proud to see in your child) and admits only two of the four have one. The rule is rituals over aspirations: start with one dimension (usually physical, because it funds the rest), design minimal rituals you can keep on bad days, and add anchors so a slip is not a fall. He warns that fit "in your head" is not fit in reality. "Health is rented, and rent is due every day."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/health, https://idvork.in/fit-in-my-head, https://idvork.in/physical-health

### Healthspan beats lifespan, and the rate of decline is up to you

Igor treats health as a savings account with mandatory withdrawals: you control how high your peak is and how fast you decline afterward. He trains for his Centenarian Olympics (lift a grandkid, walk an hour, get up off the floor, carry the groceries) and goes after metabolic health first. The 40s are where you write a self-fulfilling prophecy: "I'm getting old" skips the walk and skipping the walk makes you older, so audit the "I'm too old" reflex and refuse to pre-retire your body. Get the screenings and the glasses early rather than waiting for fear. "No one looks 50. They either look 40 because they're fighting for their life, or 60 because they gave up."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/last-year, https://idvork.in/42, https://idvork.in/elder

### Sleep sets the difficulty of the next day, so fix the wake time and use exhaustion to reset

Sleep is what makes life "easy mode," and a good night sets tomorrow's difficulty level. His main rule is a fixed wake time even when short on sleep; if under-slept he cuts caffeine and naps so tiredness pulls him to bed early, getting back on cycle through deprivation rather than catch-up. He gave up alcohol mainly for sleep. The early schedule is not heroics: he goes to bed very early and still hits snooze, and keeps it because motivation is highest in the morning and nothing else is awake. For middle-of-the-night rumination: read for 20 minutes, and if still awake get up and start the dreaded task. Needing less sleep while feeling great is a warning sign, not a win.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/sleep, https://idvork.in/insomnia, https://idvork.in/blueprint

### Exercise is for capability and energy; spend high-willpower hours on it and remove the excuses

Igor trains to be ready for whatever life throws at him, not to look good; fitness is the limit on what you can do and a source of energy. Because the gym takes willpower and willpower peaks in the morning, he spends his best hours there. He engineers away excuses: indoor cardio so weather cannot stop him, gym clothes kept at the gym, and a trainer so lazy days can switch the brain off and be told what to do (he resisted a trainer for 20 years and now says they are worth it, mostly for injury resilience). For beginners: start with cardio, keep it easier than you can manage, add intensity no more than about 10% a week. Kettlebells became his anchor for strength; biking is what makes him happy. "I do not do kettlebells so I look good in my jeans. I do kettlebells so I can wrestle bears."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/physical-health, https://idvork.in/kettlebell, https://idvork.in/bike

### Pain and tightness are signals: find what is weak, fix it upstream, progress slowly

Tightness is the symptom, not the problem, so instead of "how do I loosen this?" he asks "what is weak or unstable, and how do I strengthen it?" Massage and stretching matter only because they open a window in which strengthening works. He follows McGill for the back (stiff spine, mobile hips and shoulders, endurance through the Big 3) and the indirect approach for the shoulder (thoracic mobility, then pec and lat length, then scapular control, then rotation). Tendons do not hurt until they are damaged, so form and slow progression matter more than how a session feels, and he drops high-risk lifts whose precision cost outweighs their benefit. "Massage only lasts 20 minutes, stretching a day or 2, but strengthening lasts forever."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/physical-pain, https://idvork.in/back-pain, https://idvork.in/shoulder-pain

### Weight is arithmetic dominated by diet, and willpower is the weakest lever

Weight is calories in minus calories out; diet dominates and exercise is mostly a red herring for weight loss even though it is critical for energy. He sets a weight goal, checks it weekly and makes a monthly pledge because weight creeps. Dieting is not shameful, it shows you care. Because for him willpower dominates diet, he has no purity objection to a tool that changes appetite; the physics still applies, the tool just quiets the urge. Situational rules help: at the free hotel breakfast, remember you are an essentialist who would rather be healthy than eat a free waffle.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/diet, https://idvork.in/terzepatide, https://idvork.in/physical-health

### Energy, not time, is the real constraint, and sometimes the goal is lower arousal

"I don't have time" usually means "I don't have energy," which is good news because energy can be created as well as drained: workouts and productive days generate it, passive scrolling drains it, and caffeine is debt tomorrow's bad sleep repays. He pushes back on the always-high-energy ideal: the parasympathetic rest-and-digest state (low arousal, high pleasure) is where presence, gratitude, listening and good planning happen, and chronic activation is dysregulation, not strength. His levers are breathing patterns (long exhales to calm, long inhales to rev up, box breathing as a trained portable skill), gentle movement and touch. "Going slow isn't lazy. It's strategic."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/energy, https://idvork.in/slow, https://idvork.in/breath

### Pain is the signal, suffering is what you add, and the question is whether a thought works

Mental pain is an alarm meant to direct attention and action; suffering is the meaning we attach, and misery is an optional second layer ("this proves I'm unlovable") that is always unhelpful. He calls his synthesis Applied Buddhism: equanimity, Covey's gap between stimulus and response, and ACT's psychological flexibility are one move named three times. From ACT he takes workability (don't ask whether a thought is true, ask whether living by it moves you toward who you want to be), the choice point (notice the hook, swap the away move for a toward move), and Open, Present, Do What Matters as the shortest stuck-diagnostic. He rejects "full robot" mode: overriding feelings with will piled them up when he was younger; the better move is to let the feeling be loud and act on values anyway. Emotions are messengers for what you care about. "Pain is the signal. Suffering is what I add. Equanimity is the capacity to stop adding."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/act, https://idvork.in/mental-pain, https://idvork.in/emotions

### Mindfulness means noticing, not sitting, and it is trained like a muscle

Igor bounced off meditation for years because he thought it required sitting still with eyes closed. The reframe that freed him: the core instruction is "notice X" with openness and curiosity, which works on a run, in the car or mid-conversation. He still values formal practice as discipline (a daily 20-minute sit, box-breathing holds built like lifts). His model is engineering-flavored: concentration practice keeps the main thread from being paged out; awareness practice builds a watchdog thread, a kernel mode that observes the user-space apps (thoughts, roles, self-stories) without being any of them. He insists on grandmother mind when attention wanders and on doing less than you can so the habit survives, and he is candid that non-duality is "still gobbledygook" to him.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/siy, https://idvork.in/awareness, https://idvork.in/act

### Anxiety is the gap between expectations and reality: trade control for influence, face it, and break loops with a word

Anxiety is the difference between what you expected and what is happening, usually from believing you control things when you only influence them. The fix is to restate the outcome as "I will maximize my influence toward X," which closes the gap and increases the influence. Avoidance grows anxiety; gradual repeated exposure is the way out. His protocol: label it, state the precise threat without catastrophizing, estimate realistic probabilities, check confidence against past counterexamples, act. He separates caring (intensity of feeling) from mattering (objective impact over time). For sticky rumination loops ("psychic shadows") he first checks the pain is not a legitimate signal, analyzes it, then binds the conclusion to a single word he can say the moment the loop starts, because suppression does not work. Daily stream-of-consciousness journaling drains the rest. "Anxiety is practicing failure in advance."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/anxiety, https://idvork.in/anxiety-management, https://idvork.in/psychic-shadows

### Mood is a fast, buggy heuristic; happiness is a climate you build, not a mood you catch

Mood is the brain's instant estimate of how good the present is, biased and bidirectional (physical pain makes a relationship feel worse; smiling lifts it; a bad mood broadcast reads as criticism). He distinguishes mood (weather) from the pursuit of happiness (climate), a skill made of inner peace, passion and compassion. Happiness splits into pleasure (relief, fleeting), satisfaction (flow and hard accomplishment) and meaning (living your identity even when the doing is miserable), and he designs for the remembering self: novelty, presence, one peak, a good ending. Numbing yourself to avoid hurt slowly erodes passion, and lost passion is the road toward depression.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/mood, https://idvork.in/happy, https://idvork.in/caring

### Addiction is escape; sort activities by compulsion and impact, not by whether they look productive

Addiction is about escaping fear, shame or your own thoughts, not about substances. Two questions sort any activity: am I compelled, and is it hurting the rest of my life? Compelled and harmful is addiction; compelled and net-positive is passion (with an opportunity cost); not compelled is a hobby. The tell is how stopping would feel: relief, loss or nothing. In the moment he sets a 20-minute timer and asks what he would rather be doing. He is suspicious of virtuous-looking addictions (workaholism, success addiction, endless agent "vibing") and applies the kid-test: would you be proud to see your child do this exact thing this much? He knows his pattern: get hooked, quit cold turkey, assume it is gone; it comes back if the need underneath is never addressed. "TikTok = relief. Vibe coding = loss. Magic = nothing in particular, and that's fine."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/addiction, https://idvork.in/vibing, https://idvork.in/mind-at-work

### Anger, pride and shame are mind monsters: don't feed them, and judge yourself against yourself

Anger is an intoxicant that gives a false sense of control and has never improved one of his decisions. The graded antidotes: tolerance for frustration (how much will this matter in hours, days, months?), compassion for anger (accept the past, hope for the future), and looking for the fear behind the anger. Pride, in his loose sense of arrogance, is answered by comparing yourself to what you want rather than to peers, weighing your own principles over others' judgment, and choosing being effective over being right. Shame ("I am bad") is separated from guilt ("I did bad"); shame grows in secrecy and dissolves with empathy. "Anger is the enemy. It is an intoxicant, it causes poor judgment and makes everything worse."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/anger, https://idvork.in/pride, https://idvork.in/mind-monsters

### Extreme moods are as serious as low ones: treat feeling unusually great as a warning and plan while stable

Depression is not sadness but a lack of feeling, a veil the sufferer mistakes for truth. Euphoric high energy is equally dangerous because it feels so good, and describing it fondly is itself a trap; his tell for the best self versus an unhealthy high is whether he still gets tired and needs sleep. Write the response plan while stable and name your own highest-risk failure mode in it. For losing someone to suicide he offers the "brain attack" model from lived experience: an acute, fatal disease, not a reflection of the person's love, and a hereditary risk to take seriously rather than fear. Antidotes lean on everything else here: guard sleep, do cardio, journal, meditate, keep work boundaries, remember the story is not over.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/depression, https://idvork.in/mania, https://idvork.in/suicide

### Spiritual health is sustained motivation you'd be proud to see in your child, and it breaks in three ways

Igor is openly unsure spiritual health is "real," so he settles on a behavioral test: are you sustainably motivated in a way you would be proud to see in your child? (The child framing makes lying harder and excludes the addict.) When it fails, one of three things is missing: Direction (a north star; his is the eulogy, reviewed often enough to show up on the calendar), Scale (getting small: up, out, through time, or in), and Acceptance (influence, not control, from the Serenity Prayer, the Stoics and ACT). Emotional health gets you through the sprint, spiritual health through the marathon, and keeping them separate guards against spiritual bypassing. The biggest trap is the philosophy pit: defining meaning while still on the couch. "Stop asking life what it means. Start answering life's question about what you mean to do with it."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/spiritual-health, https://idvork.in/stoicism, https://idvork.in/health

### Religion lives in practice, not proof; a secular person has to rebuild ten jobs God does in one package

Igor lost belief through an engineering lens (no proof, no reproducibility, no mechanism) and came to value religion anyway when his community's mourning rituals carried him through a death without asking what he believed. Religions persist because they solve hard problems: what to do when someone dies, passing values down, building trust, a day of rest. Belief often follows behavior, and when it does not, the practice still works. He lists ten jobs God does (unconditional love, confidence, inner peace, cosmic perspective, locus of control, mission, moral framework, guide, ritual design, community) that a secular person must source separately. He reads devotionals and translates them line by line ("trust in God" becomes "maximize influence, not control"), recording where the translation is lossy. Each of his frictions (proof, hypocrisy, pushiness, unquestionable rules) gets a "to be fair" counterpoint. "It's like trying to run Windows software on a Linux machine - constant compatibility issues, constant translation layers."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/religion, https://idvork.in/walking-with-god, https://idvork.in/greek-orthodox

### The piece he can't cobble together alone is the weekly room full of flawed people

Igor reads the research as an uncomfortable verdict on his own plan: the flourishing benefits come from showing up in community regularly, not from belief, and "spiritual but not religious" people do worse than people who are neither. Keeping the practices and dropping God works for meditation, gratitude, daily review and memento mori, but not for the tribe. He admits he is intellectually aligned with fellowship and does not do it, names his failure modes (idealizing a group and being disappointed, or deciding he can figure everything out alone), and sets the rule: join a group for wisdom, inspiration and courage, not perfection. Finding a secular weekly gathering is his stated open question.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/spiritual-health, https://idvork.in/walking-with-god, https://idvork.in/tribe

### Midlife means switching curves: let fluid ability decline and invest in wisdom, teaching and connection

In midlife the raw horsepower you and society value declines (working memory, input bandwidth, peak load); the choice is to deny it and suffer or embrace it and optimize. Following Brooks, he aims for the second curve of crystallized intelligence: teaching, mentoring, deep relationships, spiritual practice, and kicking success addiction (defining success as having more than others) because that metric only gets harder with age. From Jung he takes the ego-inflation cycle: roles and achievements should serve the whole self, not replace it, and the job is to notice faster when he is performing his résumé rather than living it. He sees AI tools as an equalizer that favors experience.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/elder, https://idvork.in/40yo, https://idvork.in/self-ego

## Engineering management

### A manager's only product is the team, and people need effectiveness rather than efficiency

The manager has no output of his own: the team's success is the manager's success, and the product he expects to be proudest of is the people he watched grow. Success has four dimensions (business results, high-performing people and teams, engineering efficiency and process, technical assets); for outsiders he describes it as running a factory where the workers are healthy and learning, the procedures are efficient and the machines are cheap to maintain. People are three-dimensional humans, not "resources," and he is offended by managers who treat developers as fungible deliverable-producers. He measures himself by his people's career velocity, their feedback, trains running on time, and no regretted attrition, with team health before team impact before organizational influence. "I can't be efficient with people - only effective. It always pays off."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/manager-book, https://idvork.in/being-a-great-manager, https://idvork.in/what-makes-a-leader

### "No surprises" is the manager's core contract

A surprise about performance, promotion or a reorg is evidence the manager failed to set expectations, and he runs a mini root-cause analysis on himself when one happens. The mechanism is constant foreshadowing: meet often, see around corners, raise concerns early, know what each person cares about, so hard feedback lands as confirmation. He applies the same rule upward (heads up as soon as you know, bring an idea with the problem) and to engineers (share bad news early, don't go dark on a languishing problem). "If I'm doing my job, you already know where you stand. Surprises mean I failed."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/manager-book, https://idvork.in/90days

### Managers must stay technical but should not write critical-path code

Managers own technical outcomes: they attend design reviews, are on every page, follow trends, read the seminal books and keep side projects. He wants engineers to be very senior ICs before switching because technical skill decays once management takes over the day. Managers coding on the critical path get pulled into urgent work, so they can only take low-priority tasks, which is an expensive priority inversion; they should read code for spot checks, for assessing people, and during outages, and use their own product and dev tools like customers do. The AI-era update: vibe-coding prototypes and team tools is now cheap enough to be worth it, but an EM who vibe-codes all day has stopped managing.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/manager-book, https://idvork.in/ai-native-manager

### Positional authority distorts what a manager says, so discount it on purpose

The manager, not being immersed in the problem, knows the least about the tech, so his words should not be overweighted because of his title. Each promotion adds a thicker pair of gloves: more power, less dexterity, so choose words more carefully. He welcomes "hey, did you..." sanity checks from reports (they cost nothing if covered and are priceless if not), frames his own downward questions as "I'm guessing you already thought of this, but..." until trust flows both ways, and asks people not to confuse his confident manner with conviction. "Please don't confuse my confidence with my conviction, and push back."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/manager-book, https://idvork.in/manager-book-appendix

### Coaching is midwifery: ask questions so the insight comes from the other person

Coaching is the most important part of management. A handed-over solution is forgotten at best and offensive at worst, so the coach asks open questions, lets the coachee do most of the talking, and stays curious and non-judgmental. His toolkit is the seven questions: what's on your mind, what else, what's the real challenge for you, what do you want, how can I help, what will you say no to, what was most useful. He avoids "why" because it makes people defensive, matches support to the learning stage (direction when competence is low, support when commitment is low), and admits coaching is selfish because the questions he asks others are the ones he forgets to ask himself. "A coach can not give a solution, she must give birth to the insight from within the coachee."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/coaching, https://idvork.in/manager-book

### 1:1s are the highest-ROI and most expensive meeting, so keep status reports out of them

Even a tiny per-meeting improvement compounds over years, and without a forum concerns go unhandled, like a program whose default exception handler is "ignore all." He opens with "what's on your mind," coaches, and closes with "what can I do immediately to help you?" He bans the public news-and-weather status report and the deep dive that should have been a written artifact; ask for a rough brain-dump doc to comment on asynchronously instead. He pushes for the awkward conversations, groups 1:1s on the same days because they need a particular frame of mind, and suspects the 30-minute norm traded effectiveness for efficiency (hour-long 1:1s, especially remote). First-90-days 1:1s use the same questions for everyone to avoid bias.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/manager-book, https://idvork.in/remote-work, https://idvork.in/90days

### Catch people doing things right, and give feedback as your own reaction

Reinforcement comes first: immediate, observed praise, in public when possible, so the whole team learns the behavior, and more of it than feels natural, because managers think they score 8/10 on recognition while employees report 4/10. Hard feedback is hard for three reasons (it is a surprise, people don't want to hear it, they doubt you have their interests at heart), each with its own fix (foreshadowing, high trust, framing in terms they care about). He adopts first-person feedback: "here's where you started to lose me" instead of "you need to communicate better." Raising a concern about a colleague is an act of care, not throwing someone under the bus.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/manager-book, https://idvork.in/coaching, https://idvork.in/moments-at-work

### Career growth runs on written artifacts: growth plans, pre-written self-assessments, pre-written promo docs

The career growth plan looks six months to two years out, reviewed quarterly; the pre-written self-assessment covers one to six months, checked every four to eight weeks; fast feedback happens immediately. Pre-writing the self-assessment forces both sides to agree up front on whether the work matters and what its impact will be: impact, not motion. For promotions he starts by assuming the engineer's self-assessment is right, compares it with the level criteria, builds a shared gap plan with stretch projects and mentors, and pre-writes the promo document early so other managers can check it. "Gaming" a well-designed review process is mostly just doing high-impact work.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/manager-book

### Delegate the outcome, not the steps: stewardship over gopher delegation

Stewardship delegation hands over responsibility along with agreement on five things: desired results (the what, not the how), guardrails, resources, the accountability model, and the consequences. Most of his delegation failures came from the delegatee not really understanding those five, so he has them help define accountability and consequences, which also builds buy-in. Gopher delegation ("go for this, go for that") feels fast and turns the manager into a babysitter. His stated goal is to do nothing: anything he is doing that someone else should own or wants to learn, he hands off, sometimes as shadow coaching where he does part of the work and the report sends it and takes the credit.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/delegate, https://idvork.in/manager-book-appendix, https://idvork.in/manager-book

### Motivation is intrinsic (autonomy, mastery, purpose) plus deliberately engineered peak moments

Carrots and sticks fail for creative work; he uses Pink's model. Autonomy: you pick your work and own the decision even when he disagrees. Mastery: he pushes you to 110% but tells you whether you are at 90, 100 or 110. Purpose: he calls out the why and the small wins. Satisfaction and dissatisfaction are independent axes: removing irritants never creates the set-your-alarm-early feeling. Because people remember life through peaks and work has few of them, creating moments of elevation, insight and pride is part of the job (break the script, celebrate milestones and transitions, cook the team breakfast), and he fights the pull toward watered-down versions that turn moments into programs. "Moments need to be personal, not programmatic."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/moments-at-work, https://idvork.in/work-satisfaction, https://idvork.in/manager-book

### Managing out low performers is kind and necessary, as long as it is fair and never a surprise

A low performer drains the team, who cover for them, and is miserable themselves; getting them into a role where they succeed is the best outcome, and unregretted attrition is incompatibility, not the person being bad. He separates skill gaps (mis-hires, over-leveling, premature promotion, which is why promotions should lag) from consistency gaps, which are harder to document. He accepts that at large scale some outcomes will be unfair in both directions. The process runs from a quiet zone of concern to a documented pre-PIP with agreed criteria to a formal PIP. Jerk behavior is addressed immediately; communication style is judged by whether the person influences at their level.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/manager-book

### Process is organizational habit: valuable for solved problems, toxic when nobody reviews it

Process reduces variance, creates a pit of success and saves mental energy on solved problems; each process needs an owner, a success measure and a review cadence, and process created high in the org chart for one local problem then applied everywhere stifles ownership. Never have a human do what a computer can do (linters, tests, data pulled from bug systems), and AI made that 100x more true. He opposes code review as an error-catching gate: reviews should train and share knowledge, pairing is better, and quality belongs to linters and tests. He wants the manager on every page and outage, a standing engineering-excellence budget of 10 to 20% of capacity, and correction-of-errors writeups so no mistake happens twice. "There is nothing more powerful than a motivated engineer, don't let process take that away."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/manager-book, https://idvork.in/coe, https://idvork.in/bugs-gone

### One-way-door change starts at minus 100 points; pilot the two-way doors

A team can only absorb so much change, change has unexpected consequences, and the old system had solved problems nobody remembers that come back afterward. Two-way-door changes start as a pilot, spread by voluntary adoption, and carry rollback criteria. From The First 90 Days: don't change for change's sake, don't restructure your way out of deeper problems, don't overestimate how much change the org can absorb. Joining a new company, he learned to describe the problem and ask what the local solution was instead of importing the old company's answer.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/manager-book, https://idvork.in/90days

### Plan in the order why, what, when, who, and keep slack in the system

Assign people last: starting with who limits solutions to current skills, attaches people to unproven projects, and makes cancellations personal. Roadmaps are built from tracks, boulders and ROI-ordered projects with cut lines; estimates are coarse, in engineering-quarters, which shows capacity and the critical path; precision depends on horizon, and each horizon serves a different audience. Allocation maximizes the overlap of personal preference, business need and probability of success, and a senior doing junior work leaves nobody doing senior work. Anxiety is the gap between reality and expectations, and slack is how you keep the two close.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/manager-book, https://idvork.in/sustainable-work

### Escalations are priority alignment: escalate the decision, not the problem

An escalation is two teams pursuing locally correct goals that conflict, where the call belongs one level up; done well it is a sign of a healthy system, and the real failures are stalemate, resentment and flip-flopping. The healthy version lays out both perspectives and the tradeoffs and arrives with options and a recommendation. Cross-org work follows Getting to Yes and Covey: seek first to understand, aim for win/win or no deal, agree shared goals with exec buy-in, then settle how, when and who. When the team disagrees with leadership he first either believes the decision or disagrees and commits, then listens, then looks for a win/win and an agreed ripcord. "If the priorities were obvious, they wouldn't be having this conversation."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/manager-book, https://idvork.in/negotiate

### When a charter fails or a reorg lands, set a date, tell people yourself, and stay positive about the future

Ending projects and abrupt direction changes are harder than firing. When a space cannot succeed in reasonable time, declare success, wrap up, and avoid sunk-cost wishful thinking; don't keep a walking-dead project alive while hunting for a charter, pick a pivot date and hit it. For reorg announcements: write the FAQ in advance, rehearse, clear the day, tell people individually starting with those who will take it best, then a team meeting at day's end, energy low and caring, positive about the future. During layoffs he refuses to promise anyone safety, keeps follow-ups 1:1, and reminds people that those laid off are not dead, so reach out to them.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/manager-book

### In the AI era, coach people through adoption stages toward sustainable execution

AI-native means rebuilding processes on the assumption that AI exists, not bolting it on. People move through stages (Denial, Skepticism, Fear, Frenzy, Burnout, Sustainable Execution) and each transition has a coaching move: answer denial with a demo on the person's own code, skepticism with honest urgency, fear by making experimentation safe, frenzy by setting the boundaries the person will not set, burnout with permission to slow down. AI is a team sport with natural roles (trailblazer, toolsmith, skeptic, bridge), shared prompt libraries, shared failures, and AI-free zones so people keep their thinking skills and human connection. He chooses to emphasize the year of wonder over the year of chaos while being honest about both. "Fear is a necessary waypoint, not a destination."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/ai-native-manager, https://idvork.in/ai-native-onboarding

## Career, hiring, and sustainable work

### Hire for smart, gets things done, and not an asshole; AI renamed the middle one agency

Companies' competency lists reduce to three traits, each with interview probes ("teach me something you deeply understand," "tell me the hardest feedback you gave"). After AI the formula holds but the weights change: smart becomes curiosity and judgment (telling AI garbage from gold), gets-things-done becomes agency plus relentlessness, and EQ matters more because AI narrows the technical gap. Knowledge is queryable now and it is easy to look busy, so the two-hour rule: ship something after two hours of research. He prefers generalists almost always, wants a pipeline of junior hires, and favors artifact reviews and whiteboarding real systems over puzzles. "Intelligence times zero agency is zero, and multiplying by AI doesn't help."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/ai-hiring, https://idvork.in/agency, https://idvork.in/manager-book

### Recruiting is sales, ranked second only to a live site outage

His priority list is literally: a full outage, then recruiting, then everything else. Recruiting is product sales where the product is the team; great salespeople solve the customer's needs, so he starts with what the candidate values and will steer them to another team if that fits better. He works the whole funnel: public artifacts at the top, fast turnaround and magic tricks in the middle, lunches with the team and help building their dream-job decision matrix at the bottom. All selling must pass the same test: it is only legitimate if the buyer's life improves. When someone resigns: happy for you, sad for the team, do you want to be talked out of it, and "teams should be like rivers, not lakes."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/manager-book, https://idvork.in/sell, https://idvork.in/why-is-no-one-referring

### Job hunts are high-leverage and slow: over-prepare, own the schedule, don't take rejection personally

Priorities: get the best job (you will keep it about four years), get it fast, over-prepare, and treat your current job as the lowest-ROI use of energy during the hunt. Five stressors, each with a fix: lack of confidence (weeks of study, a full-size whiteboard, mock interviews), being rushed (budget about three months), lack of options (keep a list and a worst-case job; interview at less prestigious companies first), disappointing others, and pressure from the current job. Interviews are tuned to avoid false positives, so strong people get rejected; don't pick a job for superficial reasons, including a charming recruiter. "Don't leave a job, go to a job."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/job-hunt-stress, https://idvork.in/interviewsarehard, https://idvork.in/the-recruiter-does-not-think-you-are-hot

### Compare offers by total compensation over time, never by salary or projected stock gains

Salary comparisons are meaningless because companies mix base, bonus and vesting equity differently; switching forfeits unvested equity, so a large signing bonus may only make you whole. New hires land high in a band and promotions land low; watch for the four-year cliff when the initial grant finishes vesting. Never count stock appreciation, because that assumes you can predict the market. Negotiate (he has essentially never seen an offer retracted for it) and use competing offers as leverage. Comparing salaries with peers makes one person ecstatic and the other miserable, so keep pride out of it.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/comp, https://idvork.in/manager-book

### Sustainable work is a culture leaders must model, because more hours rarely help

Organizations reflect their leaders, so he models it with concrete rules: leave at 4pm four days a week, no evenings or weekends, no work email on the phone, delayed send for any off-hours mail, booked vacations with the laptop off, and proudly attending kids' events during the day. Team mechanisms: off-the-books time off, sprint capacity reduced for people under strain, meetings between 10 and 4, and late-night commits watched as a stress signal. He burned out three times and worked 60-hour weeks when young; he does not forbid that, but argues 60-hour people are usually less effective, and that some organizations will cost you if you have a life, in which case leave. Task overload is never fixed by more hours, only by prioritizing. "I don't want to die knowing I spent too much time at work and I bet you don't either."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/sustainable-work, https://idvork.in/overload, https://idvork.in/manager-book

### Judge a job on two independent axes, satisfaction and dissatisfaction, and re-roll only when the problem is structural

A job sits on a matrix of satisfaction (what gets you up early) and dissatisfaction (what ruins your sleep). High and low means you have arrived; low on both needs analysis; low satisfaction with high dissatisfaction means change; high on both is "stressed and amazing" and usually unsustainable. Before switching, check whether the problem is structural or temporary and whether the issue is you rather than the environment. People rank above problems ("if you're working with shitty people on an awesome problem, there will be much suffering"), and following a tribe you gel with across companies is a real strategy.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/work-satisfaction, https://idvork.in/job, https://idvork.in/dip

### Moving from manager to IC is a change in value proposition, not in craft: keep the caring, drop the ownership

The hard part of moving from EM to IC was not shipping code again; it was that the scorecard changed while identity was built on the old one, so shipping something good felt useless because no one grew. Two failure modes: shadow managing without authority, and pretending not to care. The resolution is to coach anyone who asks and ask questions rather than pull levers, and to give the change a couple of review cycles. Agentic tools make the IC role look like managing a fleet, so most manager skills carry over (crisp specs, delegation choices, review as the remaining quality gate); what does not carry over is the meaning, because agents do not grow or come back years later to say it mattered. "Your first month is grief wearing a productivity costume."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/em-to-ic, https://idvork.in/manager-book

### Failure is data, not a verdict about the person

Failure is an event, not a person, and a requirement for growth. The response is to analyze without judgment, write the correction of errors, and never repeat the same mistake. Failures within your control have a known fix; external setbacks call for a fixed grieving period, refocusing on your core value, and new channels to deliver it. Doing nothing is usually a failure too. Never attribute to malice what can be attributed to incompetence, and when a mentor drops a random fact, ask the follow-up question.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/fail, https://idvork.in/coe, https://idvork.in/The-Genesis-Node

## Products and persuasion

### Start from the customer experience and validate cheaply; watch what users do, not what they say

An inspiring vision and a focused strategy: one persona at a time, obsess over customers rather than competitors, stubborn on vision and flexible on details. Discovery retires four risks early (will customers buy it, can they use it, can we build it, does it work for the business). Customers and executives cannot tell you what to build, and most ideas will not work, so lean on revealed preferences and desire paths: pave the routes users already walk, and "hallucination-squat" what agents keep trying to call. Interview with The Mom Test (past specifics, no pitching). Big companies mistake process for content. Platform rule: build each iteration with a committed partner, ship the first one fast, make each iteration cheaper to change. "If you build it they will not come."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/product, https://idvork.in/job, https://idvork.in/manager-book-appendix

### Persuasion should be win/win and should lower the other person's risk

Selling is legitimate only if the buyer's life improves (win/win or no deal; "upserve, don't upsell"). Attune by reducing your own power and seeking first to understand; before any pitch know what you want people to know, feel and do. Negotiate per Getting to Yes: hard on problems, soft on people, outcomes over positions, objective criteria, know your BATNA. From his own startup he learned customers did not refer him because a referral put their credibility at risk, so remove that risk first, then supply the message, then reduce the effort. Reframes (problem to outcome, failure to feedback, critic to advisor) reopen stuck conversations; ideas that stick (simple, concrete, a story) are made, not born. The 48 Laws of Power is "a very cynical" view of the dark side, not a playbook.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/sell, https://idvork.in/negotiate, https://idvork.in/sleight-of-mouth

## Writing, making, and joy

### Writing is a thinking discipline, and bad writing is a thinking failure

Useful writing tells a reader (often yourself) something true and important they did not know, in a way that leaves no doubt; when writing is bad the cause is missing analysis, synthesis and simplification, not grammar. Writing to understand (capturing and sorting to build your own model) is different from writing to be understood (working out what the reader needs and simplifying toward it). He learned at Amazon that clear writing depends on critical thinking, which depends on critical reading, and was still weak at all three after three years there. The first question is "good for whom?", then who the audience is, what they know, what they should learn and do. He picks the form on purpose (a story for a journey; recommendation, reasons, tradeoffs and assumptions for a decision), drafts in separate phases (Madman, Architect, Carpenter, Judge), and treats the blog as his evergreen notes: atomic, concept-oriented, densely linked, revised for years. "Most writing is bad. Not due to spelling, punctuation or grammar, but due to lack of critical thinking."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/writing, https://idvork.in/vim-for-writing

### Overinvesting in hard writing is training, and AI must not do the thinking for you

You suck until you don't, and a very large writing effort is muscle-building, not waste: he spent about 80 hours on one outage writeup and counts most of it as training, which is always worth it. On AI he agrees that writing which is thinking cannot be handed off, because you construct the idea in the act of putting the words together; strength became optional, so you train it deliberately through dense reading, writing about what you read, and daily handwritten journaling. His open question is how to use AI to help you think rather than to write for you.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/writing, https://idvork.in/process-journal

### Make content for yourself and your kids first, start before you have a plan, and check it against guard rails

His early videos had no story, strategy or audience; the fix was naming each content line's purpose and audiences: himself (remembering himself at his best), his kids (leaving them his values), and the public. Feedback beats planning, because planning while afraid only feels like progress. Production stays simple: the phone you have, the editing app's defaults, drop gear that is too much work. Before posting, a checklist: kind and honest? a class act? passes the New York Times test? would I regret my kids or a future employer seeing it? helps even one person? Nothing that embarrasses family or coworkers, no hype, no clickbait. Creator monetization is a brutal power law; do it for the skill and the people, and count any money as a bonus. "Would I regret my kids or future employer seeing this?"
Evidence: https://idvork.in/content-audience, https://idvork.in/content-creation, https://idvork.in/monetize

### Producing beats consuming, but producing can become its own addiction

People are meant to produce, not consume: consumption leaves no trace and does not compound, while what you produce keeps helping strangers and builds skills that carry over. When he consumes he picks the easiest drug, ranking media by how hard it is to stop (infinite scroll worst, books easiest), and turns passive pleasure into enjoyment by adding people and memory. But shipping can become a compulsion (analytics at night, drafting during family time), so he applies his addiction test to his own output. People who share what they learn in public (evergreen notes, week notes, TILs) are his tribe even when they do not know him. "Make stuff. Make a lot of stuff. But notice when the making becomes the same scroll, just with extra steps."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/produce-consume, https://idvork.in/tribe, https://idvork.in/podcast

### A good hobby is accessible, lets you keep improving, and builds identity and connection

Everyone needs a hobby as a refuge: you cannot simply not think about work, you have to think about something else. The test has three parts: accessible (minimal props, can be done alone), supports mastery (you never top out), and grows identity and connection. A hobby differs from a passion or addiction by compulsion: you can put it down without feeling pulled back. Do not make the hobby your job, because then customers come first and if work goes badly you lose the hobby too. He learned by trial: scuba needed too much gear, squash and fencing ended in tendinitis, juggling was less portable than magic, which fits in a pocket and leaves a souvenir.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/hobby, https://idvork.in/new-skills

### Magic is the instrument of a core identity: dealing smiles and wonder to strangers

Bringing joy to others is the superpower he would pick, tied straight to his eulogy; magic, balloons, crazy shirts and "you are awesome" cards are the tools. The stories he retells are about connection, not technique: spectators keeping a signed card for years, a woman who wrote "freedom" on her card the day her divorce was final. A tiny act of magic can matter far more than the performer guesses. Joy acts on you involuntarily, needs safety and novelty, and is killed by anticipation; pleasure is relief that leaves you wanting more. His ethical line: attention management is fine when the intent is the spectator's experience and manipulation when it is personal gain. "Being a dealer of smiles and wonder is a priority in my life, and magic is my instrument."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/magic, https://idvork.in/joy, https://idvork.in/grateful

### Create wonder by designing the impossible moment and putting the spectator at the center

## Relationships, family, and community

### Lead with curiosity, not judgment, toward yourself and others

Grandmother mind is unconditional care that still steps in against self-destructive behavior. People behave badly out of suffering, so you are angry at the suffering, not the person. When he breaks one of his own rules the consequence is curiosity, not punishment: a six-minute retro asking what he got, why he wanted the rule, whether it was worth it, then classifying the slip as mindful or mindless and a right or wrong call. Judgment makes people hide the truth; curiosity draws it out. Compassion sorts by time: forgiveness for the past, acceptance for the present, hope for the future. ## Money

### Money is a tool for a better life, not a pile that makes you feel safe

He once told a planner the point of saving was a pile big enough to feel safe, and calls that a mistake. Spending well means experiences with your kids, investing in your health, buying time with people you love, giving, and avoiding fights; buying stuff ranks last. Happiness rises with the log of money, not linearly, and Parkinson's law applies to money as much as time. Aim for financial independence rather than "retirement," and if you wouldn't take a job for less pay you probably shouldn't take it. He worries about "financial diabetes," kids raised with money who never develop the restraint tight budgets build, and admits he does not know the fix. "Money is a tool. Use it to make your life better."

Skeptical of anyone who thinks they can beat professional traders, he prefers no-load broad index funds and time in market. ## Working with AI: CHOP, agents, evals, and coaching

### Rent the most expensive brain you can, standardize on one tool, then learn to spend tokens well

Model quality is the first-order variable: you can rent a middle schooler or a university student, and the top plan is trivial next to the force multiplication. He tried chasing every leapfrogging release, decided that was thrash, and standardizes on one coding agent, assuming it will catch up if it falls behind. For building AI applications the same rule applies: prove the problem with the most expensive model and optimize down to cheaper or faster models only once evals exist. Tokens are still a budget; a very expensive week of background agents taught him that subscription tokens feel weightless until overage, so advanced literacy moves from max-spend to routing each task to the minimum capable model, and the real question is whether the tokens are aimed at the highest-leverage problem. "Rent the most expensive brain you can."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/how-igor-chops, https://idvork.in/ai-developer, https://idvork.in/ai-journal

### The metric is time between interventions: get on the loop and pick the review gate by blast radius

Borrowing Tesla's self-driving metric, what matters is how often you have to take over; every friction point (a slow laptop, permission prompts, fear of letting the agent run) adds interventions and kills flow. Being in the loop (you check every step) is temporary while you learn what good looks like; being on the loop (the agent checks itself, you review at checkpoints you defined) is the operator's real job. One-way and two-way doors decide the mode: let the AI ship reversible things, read every line of irreversible ones. Pick one of three gates (output, code, or plan) and build it into permissions such as branch protection rather than relying on discipline. The named failure is hovering: half-watching costs you the supervision without giving you the attention. "'Review everything' isn't on-the-loop, it's in-the-loop wearing a tie."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/ai-operator, https://idvork.in/how-igor-chops, https://idvork.in/chop

### Voice is the primary interface, because talking changes what you tell the agent

Voice is not a faster keyboard. Talking makes you ramble and backfill, and you mention the obvious constraint that turns out to matter most, so the agent solves the problem you actually have instead of the one you wrote down. His rule every time: use voice, share intent, share success criteria. Transcription quality barely matters because agents are resilient to mangled input, though a good lapel mic still beats the built-in. Voice frees him from the desk (couch, treadmill, passenger seat), and sustained dictation is a reps-and-recovery muscle. The hardest part is remembering not to type. "When you type, the AI is solving the problem you wrote down; when you talk, the AI is solving the problem you actually have."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/ai-operator, https://idvork.in/ai-cockpit, https://idvork.in/how-igor-chops

### Code is cheap and thinking is expensive: react to drafts, throw bad runs away, ship ugly

The economics flipped: generating 400 lines costs seconds, untangling them costs scarce thinking tokens, the finite deep-thinking budget you wake up with. So let the AI make the first draft, because reacting costs far less than creating and a cheap draft makes it obvious which parts are throwaway. When a generation goes sideways, delete it and re-prompt with what you learned rather than salvaging line by line (the sunk-cost reflex of the old economics). He happily threw away a custom Telegram bot the week the platform shipped an official one: build disposable prototypes, not precious artifacts. Perfectionism is procrastination that also leaves the agent idle; ship a scored baseline, then climb. "In AI land, the economics flipped. The code is cheap; the thinking is expensive."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/ai-operator, https://idvork.in/ai-journal

### Conventions, skills and small CLIs are delegation infrastructure, held loosely

CLAUDE.md files and a shared conventions repo are how you encode context, since every session starts fresh, and good delegation to agents looks like good delegation to humans: clear expectations, context, success criteria. He practices compound engineering: run retros, write down whatever you had to explain three times, turn a prompt you have run twice into a skill, and route each learning to memory (facts), CLAUDE.md (one-line rules that fire every session) or skills (recipes). Convention changes get reviewed harder than code because a bad convention affects every future generation. He prefers small CLI tools that answer one question perfectly over long instructions or piles of MCP servers, admits this fights the Bitter Lesson, and keeps prompting alchemy short because it has a short half-life. "Honestly? I'm not convinced this stuff actually works. But until models are smart enough to just figure it out, you have to do something."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/how-igor-chops, https://idvork.in/ai-operator, https://idvork.in/chop

### The future programmer specifies and verifies, and the joy and flow move to orchestration

Most code will be AI-generated to get things done, and hand-written code becomes a hobby like woodworking ("artisanal hand-crafted commits"). The key skill becomes spec and verify, closer to PM work: Specification above Verification above Implementation above Craft, breadth above depth. It is the revenge of the SDET: the testers once looked down on now hold the bottleneck skill. Orchestration skills (conventions, delegation, verification) will outlast implementation skills, and reducing friction is a meta-skill that transfers. He rejects the claim that CHOP kills joy or flow; mastering the new craft is its own joy, like the move from assembly to high-level languages, while taking seriously the counterpoint that this mode is all game show and no hammock. "The meta-skill is knowing which investments compound and which depreciate."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/how-igor-chops, https://idvork.in/chop

### Agents are slow, so run several, but the human interface becomes the bottleneck

The math only works once you saturate your attention across several agents, since agents take minutes and you decide in seconds; running three flips the bottleneck from you to them. Parallelism makes you an air traffic controller with no radar, so he builds a cockpit: voice, a fast pane picker, physical buttons, an agent dashboard, GitHub triage views, and Tailscale so every agent-launched server is just a URL. He is candid about the costs (wrong commands to wrong agents, merge hell, "wiped out by 11 AM") and advises defaulting to one strong agent with clear specs, adding agents only when ownership separates cleanly. The specific tools are held loosely; building a human interface for AI delegation is the permanent skill.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/ai-cockpit, https://idvork.in/chop, https://idvork.in/ai-operator

### Act as the M2: delegate the orchestration itself and brief agents like a colleague

He maps agent organizations onto plain management vocabulary rather than Mad Max metaphors: he is the M2 (direction, review, taste), an AI manager is the M1 that dispatches persistent staff and ephemeral workers. Most people running agents are stuck doing the M1's job, dispatching every task and reviewing every diff, which strangles them at five agents. Routing heuristic: wide work with a clear spec goes through the AI manager; deep work where precision matters goes straight to the individual agent, because the manager filters intent. A brief should prove you understood the problem (file paths, what to change, how you will know), and "implement the plan" pushes synthesis onto the agent. Even ephemeral workers get human names, because hex hostnames make status reports unreadable. "The subagent starts with none of your context. Brief it like a smart colleague who just walked into the room."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/wally, https://idvork.in/ai-operator

### Orchestration earns its keep only when the work is plural and carries judgment

A city of agents is not a smarter agent; it is the layer that makes work durable (beads, molecules, a controller) so many agents can pick it up and hand it off without babysitting. His receipt: the first workflow cost about \$9 and 32 premium-model turns for a 13-line change that a cron job should have done, and what sold him was an agent reading a large diff, seeing it was build-artifact noise, and refusing to open the PR. Match the altitude of the tool to the altitude of the decision: expensive models and peer review for judgment, plain scripts for plumbing, and no extra layers for one-shot work (cargo-cult orchestration). Work, not roles, is the primitive, and model choice stays a config value. From bring-up: trust the runtime over the doctor, and require every tool in a probabilistic stack to ship `doctor` and `repair`. "Match the altitude of the tool to the altitude of the decision."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/gas-city, https://idvork.in/gas-city-rig, https://idvork.in/gas-city-home

### Build your own claws, and accept that most of the work is goop with a two-week half-life

A claw is a persistent, named AI with memory, natural messaging and autonomous action; the jump from agent to claw is the jump from tool to colleague, like training a Pokémon team that grows as you invest. He runs three (life, work, car) and builds his own instead of installing someone else's for three reasons: his data stays on his hardware under his own security model, the building is the point, and serious claws need heavy customization anyway. Claws are about 80% goop (bridges, pipelines, configs) that infrastructure will eat within weeks; he writes it anyway and is happy when the platform obsoletes it. Claw-filed work is signed so readers know which entity did it, and he admits the system is a Winchester Mystery House he cannot stop adding wings to. "The jump from agent to claw is the jump from tool to colleague."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/igors-claws, https://idvork.in/claw, https://idvork.in/larry

### Evals, not prompts, are the moat, and testing is now the bottleneck skill

How you know the code is right matters far more than the code, since vibed code is correct by evolution, not deliberate design, and breaks under out-of-context changes. For AI applications the secret sauce is evals, then training data; the prompt has no economic value. AI can replace a well-defined role but not an unbounded problem. When changing model behavior reach for the harness first, then RAG, and post-training last, pushing change as far toward runtime as possible. His most quoted rule: if the AI writes the code and you do the manual testing, you have hired yourself as the junior tester, so make the AI the tester too and let tests serve as the specification. "If you notice AI writing the code, and yourself doing the manual testing - you've outsourced the junior developer role, but hired yourself as the junior tester."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/chop, https://idvork.in/ai-developer, https://idvork.in/ai-training

### In eval-driven hill climbing you write the fitness function and the agent climbs, but you check the mountain

When an agent runs the greedy-search loop, your role collapses to defining the fitness function and seeding a baseline, then judging the plateau. An eval is a scoring rule plus a frozen test set: the rule must be cheap, unambiguous, aligned, weighted right and decomposable, and the test set must provoke every failure mode. Hand-score three outputs before spending budget and log the trajectory; when results disappoint, suspect the eval first, usually the test set. Hill climbing cannot tell you you are on the wrong mountain: six attempts improved a chroma-key pipeline 65x, and a penny-per-image service solved the real problem. Afterwards the winner becomes infrastructure and the eval a regression guard; ship the best checkpoint, not the last one. "You set the mountain; the agent walks up it."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/hill-climbing, https://idvork.in/ai-testing, https://idvork.in/ai-journal

### Test the agent inside its harness, with deterministic checkers first and judges second

Prompt-in, completion-out is the wrong unit in the agentic era; the thing under test is model plus tools plus permissions, and swapping the harness changes the score, the same way you judge a person in an environment. He wants eval tools that run an agent in a workdir and use his own tools as oracles, runs saved immutably so new graders can re-score history, and local files rather than a SaaS dashboard. LLM judges are opt-in for quality and work better pairwise than on abstract scales; averaging judges only helps if they disagree. Test the graders with a known-good and a known-sloppy mock run. His first real eval showed a weak config failed by giving up at the permission envelope, not by editing wrong. "If your checkers have never failed a bad run on purpose, you don't know that they work."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/ai-testing, https://idvork.in/ai-eval-tools

### Verification, progress and cost bound how dark the factory can go, and humans own the boundary

A fully AI-run dark factory is correct inside a box (easy zone, cheap verification, small bill) and breaks at whichever of three limits binds first. Verification is the one everyone names. Progress is the one people miss: as a codebase gets more coupled it moves from easy zone to complexity wall to complexity collapse, where no move improves the whole. Cost is the one nobody understands yet. Humans add value at the edges: the oracle where tests cannot encode taste, redrawing the architecture so a good next move exists, and deciding when to regenerate instead of fix. Review becomes more expensive than creation, so AI PRs should show their verification (screenshots, test counts, preview links), and code no longer signals understanding or commitment. "The factory owns the interior. You own the boundary."
Evidence: https://idvork.in/dark-factory, https://idvork.in/ai-native-vocab, https://idvork.in/explainers

### Fast tokens are great for shallow, checkable work, but a bigger thinking budget does not buy correctness

Ultra-fast inference makes real-time uses possible, and slow models make you lose patience, so it is a tradeoff between smart and fast, solved either with many background agents or with faster models that keep getting smarter. His benchmark found fast providers nailed easy tasks in under two seconds but confidently returned wrong sudoku grids; a larger reasoning budget did not predict correctness, and headline time-to-first-token can hide a seven-minute wait for the answer. Rule: use speed where you can cheaply verify, and for hard work pick a model with a track record and a provider that leaves it room to finish. Build a grader for the grader, because his own parser once flattered the story he wanted to tell.
Evidence: https://idvork.in/ai-speed-vs-thinking, https://idvork.in/ai-inference, https://idvork.in/ai-developer

### Isolate the agent, and assume your own security setup is misconfigured until verified

Agents run in YOLO mode only inside disposable containers (cattle, not pets) under a separate GitHub identity that can only open PRs, so a feral agent's blast radius is one container. Willison's lethal trifecta (private data, untrusted content, external communication) is the core danger of claws: the power and the danger are the same thing. He publishes his own failures as lessons: agents pushed straight to main because a branch-protection ruleset was disabled ("a bank vault door on a tent"), a live API key sat in a public repo for a year via auto-captured chat logs, and an AI reviewer approved a PR that silently deleted a post. Rules: chat logs leak secrets, so filter them; least privilege is about the target, not the verb; verify your assumptions. Post-hoc retraction layers on models are safety theater. "Defense in depth only works if all the defenses are actually... defending."

### A named AI coach works because it is a mirror, not an advisor

He gives his AI coach a name because humans talk to people, not dashboards: "talk to Larry" is a conversation and harder to blow off than "review my metrics," and the name is honest, a name rather than a disguise. The value is context (journals since 2011, weekly reports, health data) and closing the loop: reviewing what happened against what he intended matters more than planning. The technique is the trampoline prompt, reflecting his own words back harder ("you've committed to restart meditation 5 times") so the insight comes from him. Tony, a Soprano-voiced persona trained on his eulogy values, is the same mirror with a Jersey accent. "Like the Hair Club for Men: I'm not only building it, I'm also a client."

### The second brain is a context layer fed by pre-brief and debrief, not a filing system

Classic personal knowledge management failed because organizing is a librarian's job nobody wants; with AI as librarian, the bottleneck becomes whether you have rich enough raw material. The recipe is available context (pull data out of silos, which is why he built Context Grabber), a pre-brief before acting and a debrief afterwards, forming a flywheel with no separate maintenance step. Personas (the life coach, the car coach, the reading assistant) are users of the second brain, not the brain: the second brain is the investment and the personas are the dividends. The gaps: fragile memory, no unified layer, privacy tension, manual capture. "Your brain is for thinking, not storage."

### Companion AI needs engineered friction, or it quietly hollows out your human relationships

Human connection matters, AI is already rated more compassionate than people, and the danger is the frictionless path: vulnerability is a muscle that atrophies, persistent memory drifts you into a private dialect because the AI removes the human-confusion gradient that calibrates your speech, and your spouse gets the leftovers of thoughts already metabolized with the AI. Levers: make companions disagree by design, use AI as triage not treatment, rehearse hard conversations with it and then have them with people, protect your vulnerability budget, and keep human-only rituals. For tool AI frictionlessness is the goal; for companion AI it is the trap. "A chatbot will never need you. A friend will, and that's the gift."

### AI flows downhill to your strongest role, so point it at the roles you want to grow

His AI policy is not "use less AI"; AI is his biggest force multiplier, but left alone it flows to the builder role he has already maxed. Three tests: whose role does it grow (father, friend, magician)? build or use (will I still use this in 30 days, or is the dopamine in the building)? when (the margins; the phone goes down when someone he is there for is awake)? Thinking and editing in his own voice are yes; AI generating the idea is no; another enabling-environment tool is no. Effortless production collapses into consumption that leaves no footprint, so he keeps deliberate friction like handwritten morning journaling, and even thinking with the coach has a margin limit because it can replace living. ### Use AI to think better, not to skip the thinking

Writing is thinking, and the struggle AI lets you skip was doing the work. CHOW (chat-oriented writing) means partnering, not outsourcing: the artifact is a side effect and clarity is the product. Play to the asymmetry that filtering is easier than creating: ask for five options and apply taste, and for creative or judgment calls have AI widen the option space (a click-to-pick sheet of 35 raccoons) because a single "best" answer is arbitrary. He measures sessions by the ratio of trampoline prompts to delegation prompts and wants at least a third trampolines. The egg theory: good AI workflows leave meaningful human contributions (architecture, real review, creative direction) so people feel ownership. AI slop is low content density, and human sludge is just as bad. "Use AI to generate options, but force myself to evaluate and choose. The filtering is human work."

### Explainers are the best use of cheap code, and the antidote to cognitive debt

AI made interactive explainers trivially easy to build (about the effort of a blog post) and critically necessary, because agents write code faster than teams can understand it, and that cognitive debt is how you get software you are afraid to change. The Willison pattern: when AI builds something you do not understand, ask it to build an interactive explanation and play with it until it clicks; if it cannot explain what it built in a picture, that is a red flag. Pair explainers with proof-of-work artifacts that verify AI claims, because AI lies confidently rather than maliciously. Explainers are in the 10% of AI output that is not slop, and the best have a narrative arc. "Text gives you information. Explainers give you understanding."

### Hyper-personalization was always wanted and only cost-limited, but SaaS still lives

Personal attention (a private chef, tutor, physician) was always desired and gated by human attention scaling one-to-one; AI drops the marginal cost to a query and reaches dimensions no price bought before. Online, the arc runs from one front page to popularity to cohorts to per-user ranking to per-user generation, with author agents and reader agents negotiating explanations guarded by a fidelity check against an echo chamber for one. For software it means tools for an audience of one, but with limits: software rots and you are its only maintainer, you are not an expert in the solution space, and DIY fits narrow low-stakes tools, not payroll or security. People also want shared experiences. "The frontier isn't 'what a rich person had', it's something that didn't exist at any price."
