If you're thinking about suicide, or the thought has even just crossed your mind, this post is for you. Take it seriously. Not wait-and-see-if-it-goes-away seriously: tell-someone-today seriously. I think of a suicidal crisis as a brain attack. Like a heart attack, it's an acute medical emergency, and you wouldn't sit alone with chest pain hoping it clears up.

The good news: this is treatable. There are therapies built for exactly this, and they work. First, though:

## You're not a bother

Here's the deal. You might think you'd be a bother. That reaching out will make someone feel bad. I've got great news for you: I'm like a robot at my core. You can't hurt me by asking. And if I get to help, I'll feel absolutely incredible. There's no greater gift you could give me.

That voice telling you you're a bother? That's the brain attack talking, not you. And a brain attack is acute: it peaks, and it passes. Your job is to get through the next hour with someone on the line.

- In the US, call or text **988** (the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline), or chat at [988lifeline.org](https://988lifeline.org/). Free, confidential, 24/7.
- In Seattle and King County, [Crisis Connections](https://crisisconnections.org/programs/crisis-services/) answers 24 hours a day at **866-427-4747**.
- Anywhere else, [findahelpline.com](https://findahelpline.com/) lists free crisis lines in 175+ countries.
- If you're in danger right now, call 911 or your local emergency number.

## There are really good treatments

This isn't "go talk about your feelings and hope." Researchers have built therapies aimed straight at suicidal thoughts and tested them in real trials. The evidence is strongest for the first one; the rest have good studies behind them too.

- **CBT for suicide prevention.** About ten sessions of cognitive therapy focused on the thoughts and moments that lead up to a crisis. In a [randomized trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16077050/), people who got it were about half as likely to make another attempt over the next 18 months, and the [2024 VA/DoD guideline](https://www.healthquality.va.gov/guidelines/MH/srb/) suggests it for people who've been through a recent crisis.
- **A safety plan.** You and a clinician write down, on one page, your warning signs, what helps you ride it out, who to call, and how to make your space safer. The best-known version is the [Stanley-Brown Safety Plan](https://suicidesafetyplan.com/). In a [study across VA emergency rooms](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29998307/), people who left with one, plus follow-up phone calls, had less suicidal behavior over the next six months and were more likely to show up for care.
- **DBT (dialectical behavior therapy).** Skills for when emotions get unbearable: how to get through the moment without making it worse, and how to turn the intensity down. In [Marsha Linehan's trial](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16818865/), people in DBT made fewer suicide attempts than people seeing expert therapists using other approaches. (That trial was in women with borderline personality disorder.)
- **CAMS (Collaborative Assessment and Management of Suicidality).** You and the clinician work out together what's driving the thoughts, then treat those drivers. A [meta-analysis of nine studies](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33998028/) found it lowered suicidal thoughts and raised hope more than the treatments it was compared against.
- **Medication, when an illness is underneath.** Depression, bipolar disorder and schizophrenia are treatable, and treating them matters. Some medications have specific evidence on suicide risk; clozapine, for example, is [FDA-approved](https://www.accessdata.fda.gov/drugsatfda_docs/label/2025/019758s107lbl.pdf) for reducing suicidal behavior in schizophrenia. Which one fits is a psychiatrist's call, not a blog's.

The first step is the same for all of them. Tell a doctor or therapist, in plain words: "I've been having thoughts of suicide." Then ask about a safety plan. If you don't have anyone to tell yet, call 988 and start there.
