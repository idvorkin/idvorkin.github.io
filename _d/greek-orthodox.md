Six months ago I couldn't tell you the difference between Catholic and Christian; now I casually drop words like theosis and the Great Schism. When someone you love finds something meaningful, you learn about it. These are my notes: the good stuff, the hard parts, and what maps to things I already care about.

## How It Differs from Protestant Christianity

### Theology: Mystery over Explanation

Protestant preaching tends toward explanation: here's what God wants, here's how you get saved, here are the verses. Orthodoxy goes the other way. Its apophatic theology holds that God is so far beyond us you can mostly say what God is _not_: not limited, not created, not fully knowable. The sacraments are called the Holy Mysteries. You aren't expected to understand them, only to take part.

The goal differs too. Protestants emphasize salvation, a single event. Orthodoxy emphasizes theosis, a lifelong process of becoming more like God (more below).

### Worship: The Body, Not Just the Mind

A Protestant service is mostly a sermon, some music and a pew. The Divine Liturgy works on every sense. Icons cover the walls, and the Orthodox venerate them, not worship them, as windows onto the saints. Incense stands for prayers rising. The service is chanted, and it has changed little in 1,500 years. You stand for much of it; many Orthodox churches have no pews. You cross yourself with three fingers, right shoulder before left, the reverse of Catholics.

Baptism is full immersion, three times. What surprised me: infants take communion right after. You don't need to understand a mystery to be part of it.

### Calendar and Fasting

Orthodox Easter (Pascha) is calculated with the Julian calendar, so it often falls days or weeks after Western Easter.

Orthodox fasting is about _what_ you eat, not _whether_ you eat. On a fast day you drop meat, dairy and eggs; on stricter days fish, wine and oil go too. Bread, vegetables, fruit, grains, legumes and, oddly, shellfish stay on the menu. The point isn't suffering. It's loosening your grip on comfort.

| Fast                 | When                              | How strict                                                                       |
| -------------------- | --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Great Lent           | ~7 weeks before Pascha            | Strictest: no meat, dairy, fish, wine or oil on many days; Holy Week most of all |
| Nativity Fast        | Nov 15 – Dec 24                   | Moderate: fish on most days, stricter toward Christmas                           |
| Apostles' Fast       | Monday after All Saints – June 28 | Lighter: fish on most days; 1–6 weeks depending on Pascha                        |
| Dormition Fast       | Aug 1–14                          | Strict, like Lent                                                                |
| Wednesdays & Fridays | Year-round                        | No meat or dairy                                                                 |

That adds up to roughly 180–200 fast days a year. Simplicity is the default and feasting the exception, and the feasts are big. Lent ends at Pascha with lamb, red eggs, pastries and wine, and it all hits harder because you went without.

## Theosis: The Orthodox Path to Becoming

This is the part that grabbed me. Theosis is the point of Orthodox Christianity, and when I looked closely I realized I'd been running a secular version of it my whole adult life without knowing the word.

### What Theosis Actually Means

Theosis (θέωσις) means deification. Not becoming God, which is heresy, but sharing in God's nature. Athanasius put it bluntly: _"God became man so that man might become god."_

No altar call or single prayer gets you there. It takes a lifetime of fasting, prayer, liturgy, stillness and community, and it's measured by whether your character actually changes, not whether you followed the rules. Orthodoxy doesn't see people as guilty and needing a pardon. It sees them as unfinished. You're made in the image of God, and your life's work is growing into his likeness.

### Theosis vs. Protestant Salvation

|                    | Protestant Salvation               | Orthodox Theosis                                 |
| ------------------ | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------ |
| **Metaphor**       | Courtroom: guilty, then pardoned   | Hospital: sick, then healed over time            |
| **Mechanism**      | Accept Jesus, saved (event)        | Lifelong transformation (process)                |
| **Human problem**  | Guilt from sin                     | Incompleteness, mortality                        |
| **What changes**   | Your legal status before God       | Your actual nature and character                 |
| **Role of effort** | Faith alone (works can't save you) | Synergy: God's grace plus your effort            |
| **Completion**     | At the moment of faith             | Never fully complete in this life                |
| **Daily practice** | Bible study, prayer, fellowship    | Fasting, liturgy, prayer, confession, asceticism |

### Theosis vs. Secular Self-Improvement

I've spent years building systems for becoming a better person: [affirmations](/affirmations), the [four healths](/health), daily rituals, [7 habits](/7h-concepts). Theosis has the same shape. You're never done. Daily practice, not a single decision, is what changes you. It involves the whole person, body included. And you need a picture of who you're becoming to grow toward.

## Stuff I Like

**You're not doing it alone.** In secular self-improvement the engine is you: your discipline, your willpower, and your fault when it fails. In theosis God is an active partner, and grace adds to your effort. When the tank is empty, there's somewhere to refuel besides "try harder."

**A humility floor.** You become like God, but you never _are_ God. Secular self-improvement can drift into optimizing yourself as an end in itself. Orthodoxy blocks that by design.

**It doesn't push religion on others.** No knocking on doors, no altar calls. The attitude is "the door is open if you're drawn to it." [Pushiness is one of my hard parts](/religion#pushiness) with religion, and Orthodoxy mostly sidesteps it.

**Confession as letting go.** You name the thing, release it and move on. What you can't accept sits in you like [psychic weight](/psychic-weight) and gets heavier over time; confession is the practice of putting it down. It's what I've been learning about [compassion](/curious), seeing yourself clearly without judgment, made into a regular practice with a priest. That might work better than doing it alone in your head.

**The vocabulary is fun to say.** Orthros, vespers, theosis, Pascha, Theotokos, chrismation, autocephalous. It all sounds like it belongs in an ancient library. When my son says "we have vespers tonight," it makes me smile every time.

## Stuff That Would Make It More Compatible With Me

**You can't question anything.** This is my biggest friction. Orthodox theology comes as a package: Holy Tradition, the church fathers, the councils, the liturgy. There's no "I like this part but not that part," and picking it apart is seen as misunderstanding what the church _is_.

My engineer brain runs on "but why?" and "show me the evidence." [Arbitrariness and literalism](/religion#arbitrariness--literalism) are already hard parts of religion for me, and Orthodoxy turns them up to eleven. Protestantism at least has a tradition of individual interpretation. In Orthodoxy the answer to "why?" is often "the church has held this for 2,000 years; who are you to know better?"

I get the logic. If the Holy Spirit has guided the church since the beginning, questioning the output means questioning the Spirit. But from the outside, accepting it kit and caboodle feels like turning off the part of my brain I trust most.
