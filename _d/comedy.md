I'm not a naturally funny writer. I'm a magician who gets laughs with props, and a guy who draws a [weekly comic strip](/the-den) that, for the first dozen strips, mostly wasn't funny. So I did what I do with anything I'm bad at: I went looking for the models. It turns out people have been taking jokes apart for two thousand years, and the pieces are reusable. This page is the models I found useful, each in a paragraph, and then the checklist I actually run before a strip gets drawn.

## Why anything is funny

Nobody has one theory that covers everything from a pun to a pie in the face. But four or five of them cover most of it, and each one tells you something different to check.

### Incongruity, then the fix

The oldest working theory. Kant called laughter "the sudden transformation of a strained expectation into nothing": you were braced for one thing, you got another, and the tension drains out of you as a laugh. Schopenhauer's version is the gap between a concept and the real thing it was supposed to cover. Modern versions add a second step: Suls' two-stage model says you first detect the incongruity and then resolve it, finding the rule that makes the surprise make sense after all. A non sequitur is incongruous and not funny. A joke is incongruous and then, a half second later, obvious. The laugh is the click of the resolution. ([Theories of humor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theories_of_humor); the two-stage model in [Frontiers in Communication, 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/communication/articles/10.3389/fcomm.2021.758173/full).)

### Benign violation

Peter McGraw and Caleb Warren's theory is the one I use most, because it tells you what's wrong when a joke doesn't land. Something is funny when it violates how the world ought to be, the violation is also benign, and you see both at once. Tickling is the ancestor: an attack that is also safe. They found three things that make a violation benign: an alternative norm that says it's fine, weak commitment to the norm being broken, and psychological distance. Push on each and you can predict the failure. Too safe and it's not a violation, so nothing happens. Too real and it's not benign, so people wince. The joke lives in the overlap, and the overlap is different for every audience, which is why the same line kills with friends and dies with the in-laws. ([McGraw & Warren, Psychological Science 2010](https://leeds-faculty.colorado.edu/mcgrawp/pdf/mcgraw.warren.2010.pdf); [overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benign_violation_theory).)

### Superiority and relief

Two older theories that are wrong as explanations of everything and right about a lot. Superiority (Plato, Aristotle, Hobbes) says we laugh at someone being lower than us, including our past selves. Mel Brooks put it best: "Tragedy is if I'll cut my finger. Comedy is if you walk into an open sewer and die." Relief (Spencer, then Freud) says laughter is tension leaving the body, which is why a scary movie gets a laugh on the jump cut and why a long setup pays off bigger than a short one. Neither tells you how to write a joke, but both tell you where the energy comes from: somebody has to lose something, and the audience has to have been holding its breath. ([Theories of humor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theories_of_humor); the Brooks line traced by [Quote Investigator](https://quoteinvestigator.com/2026/01/24/comedy-manhole/).)

### The mechanical encrusted on the living

Henri Bergson's 1900 essay has one idea I can't stop seeing: we laugh at "something mechanical encrusted on the living." A person who keeps walking after the floor is gone. A clerk who applies the rule when the rule has obviously stopped making sense. Laughter, for Bergson, is society's corrective: it punishes rigidity. If you write about machines that act like people, or people that act like machines, this is your whole premise. It's why a literal-minded assistant that does exactly what you said is funnier than a clever one. ([Bergson, _Laughter_, on Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/4352).)

### Humor as debugging

The engineer's theory. Hurley, Dennett and Adams argue in _Inside Jokes_ that the brain makes fast, cheap guesses about the world all day, that some of those guesses are wrong, and that mirth is the reward for catching one before it costs you. A joke plants a wrong assumption you don't notice, then exposes it. The pleasure is the debugger finding the bad commit. That's why a joke can't be explained into being funny: the catch has to happen in your head, not mine. And it's why the audience has to do the last step themselves. ([Boston Globe on _Inside Jokes_](https://www.bostonglobe.com/ideas/2011/11/20/why-our-brains-make-laugh/l0OWxVcnRpzfyIheFgab5N/story.html); [the book](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/9868380-inside-jokes).)

## How a joke is built

Theories explain. These are the tools.

### Two stories, one connector

Greg Dean's structure for a stand-up joke is the most mechanical description I've found, which is why it's useful. The setup makes the audience assume a first story. Somewhere in that setup is a connector, a word or thing that can be read two ways. The punchline forces a reinterpretation of the connector, and a second story snaps into place that was there the whole time. "Got a lighter?" Then: "Got a watch?" The connector was why he wanted the lighter. Writing the joke backwards works: pick the assumption you want to break, find the connector that supports both readings, write the setup so it only suggests the first. ([Greg Dean, _Step by Step to Stand-Up Comedy_](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/213045); Scotty Meltzer's [walkthrough of the structure](https://www.juggle.org/be-funnier-with-scotty-meltzer-nice-structure/).)

### The rule of three

Two items make a pattern; the third breaks it. Ken Levine, who wrote for _M\*A\*S\*H_ and _Cheers_, has the practical notes: keep the first two straight, because a funny item in the setup eats the punchline; make the pattern unmistakable; and break it without going to a non sequitur ("the Giants, the Tigers, and poi" fails because nothing connects). Specific beats generic every time, and a funny-sounding word beats a plain one, as long as the audience knows it. ([Ken Levine on the rule of threes](https://kenlevine.blogspot.com/2017/07/the-comedy-rule-of-3s.html); [Rule of three](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rule_of_three_(writing)>).)

### The game, and heightening

From improv, and the Upright Citizens Brigade manual in particular. The game of the scene is the first unusual thing that happens, and the scene is about that one thing. You don't add a second weird idea. You ask "if this is true, what else is true?" and make the first one bigger, more consequential, more absurd, with the characters playing it straight the whole way. That's heightening. Most scenes that die don't die because the idea was bad; they die because the second beat repeated the first instead of raising it. ([Game of the scene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_of_the_scene).)

### Yes, and

The other improv rule. Accept what the scene has given you and add to it. The point for writing isn't politeness, it's that denial kills momentum: a character who says "that's not real" ends the game, and a character who says "right, and so" escalates it. In a strip, the straight man's job is to yes-and the premise with a bigger question, never to argue with it. ([Yes, and...](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yes,_and...).)

### Callbacks

A callback is a joke that refers to one you told earlier, ideally in a new context. It works because the audience finishes the thought before you do, and because it rewards them for having paid attention. Do it too often and it becomes a running gag, which is a different, blunter tool. The best callback is to something the audience had forgotten was loaded. ([Callback](<https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Callback_(comedy)>).)

### Truth and pain

John Vorhaus' shorthand: comedy is truth and pain. The truth is something the audience already half-believes; the pain is the exaggeration that makes it safe to say out loud. His comic premise is the gap between the real world and the comic world, which is Snoopy as a WWI flying ace. If you can't say in one sentence what's true and who it hurts, you have a situation, not a joke. ([_The Comic Toolbox_](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/596322).)

## How I use it

This is the part that's mine. The Den is a four-panel strip about a raccoon running an AI staff, and after fourteen of them I had enough that worked and enough that didn't to write down the difference. Every example here is a strip on [the Den page](/the-den), so you can check me.

### The shape

Panel 1 states the deal. Panel 2 is usually silent and shows the deal going wrong, bigger. Panel 3 raises the stakes or asks the straight question. Panel 4 turns. I speak the straight line; Larry, the assistant, closes in one beat.

Read that against the models. Panel 1 plants the assumption (Dean). Panels 2 and 3 heighten the one unusual thing (UCB). Panel 4 is the reinterpretation, and it costs me something small (benign violation). Larry is the mechanical encrusted on the living, every single week (Bergson).

### Patterns that landed

1. **Larry's closer is one beat.** "SEEN." (#12). "YES." (#6). "ZERO BUGS. BEST WEEK YET." (#11). "CAUSE OF GYM: OUTAGE." (#4). Six words or fewer, one sentence, no "because." The reader finishes the thought.
2. **Literal compliance.** Larry does exactly what I said, which is not what I meant. "Everything you say is mail" means a sneeze gets a receipt (#12). "One task, I'll orchestrate" means fifty tasks and the typo still there (#2). "Run the tests on every commit" means the tests take the credit (#10). The reader does the translation; nobody explains it.
3. **The bill comes back to me.** The ending costs me something specific and small. I went to the gym because of an outage (#4). Nothing got done because I fell asleep before answering (#14). My safety check greps for the flag (#3). Larry never pays; Larry is serene.
4. **Larry is wrong on purpose and cannot be argued with.** The reply is true in a way I can't rebut: "YOU NEVER ANSWERED MY QUESTION." (#14), "THEY GOT A JOB. NOW THEY WANT CREDIT." (#10). Polite and plausible is the enemy. A reply I could reasonably accept is not a punchline.
5. **I win only by sidestepping.** When I get the last word, it's because I refuse the expected question: "I WANT A PICTURE OF HIM." (#8), "DON'T YOU DARE." (#1). I never win an argument with Larry; I change the game.
6. **Helpfulness is the escalation.** Larry's kindness makes it worse: "WANT ME TO START THE BOOK?" (#13), "SHALL I ASSIST?" (#1). Larry is never sorry.
7. **Show the state, then cut the line that explains it.** In #14 the picture carries the sleep (bed, Zs, then a cobweb on a frozen Larry), so the last balloon stays short. A state (asleep, frozen, gone, buried) is drawn, not said.
8. **The real quote is the best line.** If the thing that actually happened came with a verbatim line, build the strip around it. Reality is a better joke writer than I am.
9. **No homework.** A joke that needs git, tokens or model names to land fails on the blog. #10 puts the domain on a prop (a commit log reading AUTHOR: TEST) and gives the closer a line that works without it. A stranger has to get it.
10. **The biggest thing in panel 4 is the joke.** The knee-deep paper in #13, the empty list held up in #14, the raised SEEN flag in #12. When the one visual event is a small thing in the background, the panel reads as talk.

### How the soft ones failed

- **The reader is ahead of me.** Panel 1 shows the whole joke and panel 4 is me noticing it three panels later, with a warm, plausible closer that costs nobody anything.
- **Panel 3 says the punchline.** It sets up panel 4 so completely that panel 4 only demonstrates it, often with the same staging.
- **Repetition, not escalation.** Panels 2 and 3 say the same thing twice; the closer explains itself in two sentences; my panel 4 line is my panel 2 line again.
- **Domain punchline.** Funny only if you know what a git author, a token or a context window is.
- **A wisecrack about the mechanism** instead of the pile the reader is looking at.

The common thread: a premise is not a punchline. If the last panel restates the premise, demonstrates the one before it, or gives a reply I could reasonably accept, the strip is a setup with no turn.

### The pre-flight checklist

I answer each of these in one line before anything gets drawn. A "no" on 1 through 5 means rewrite.

1. **The turn.** Say panel 4 in one sentence. Is it new information, or me noticing what the reader saw in panel 1?
2. **The giveaway.** Read panel 3 alone. Can you guess panel 4 from it? If yes, blunt panel 3: a flat "DEAL." or a question with no hint.
3. **One beat.** Is Larry's closer one sentence, six words or fewer, with no "because"? If it needs a second sentence to be understood, the picture isn't doing its job.
4. **The bill.** Who pays in panel 4, and what exactly do they lose? Name it. "Nobody" means no joke.
5. **Wrong on purpose.** Would I be right to accept the panel 4 reply? If it's polite and plausible, it's soft. Larry must be literal, serene, and un-arguable.
6. **Setup pays off.** List every named prop and beat in panels 1 to 3 (a bow, a sleeping robot, a clock). Each one pays off in panel 4 or gets cut.
7. **Escalation.** Does panel 3 make panel 2 bigger, or say it again?
8. **Picture change.** Is panel 4's picture different from panel 3's, and is the biggest thing in it the joke?
9. **No homework.** Can a stranger read it with no knowledge of tokens, git, models or the tools involved? If one term is needed, it goes on a prop, not in the closer.
10. **The real words.** Is there a verbatim quote from what actually happened? Use it as the straight line or the closer.
11. **Reading order.** Does the punchline balloon read last? Three balloons in a panel need the staircase rule.
12. **Privacy, line by line.** Every balloon: nothing about work, health, family, career or a journal. The AI machinery misbehaving is the only subject.
13. **Honor the ending.** Read every earlier line against panel 4. Does any line ask for the thing panel 4 throws away?

### The one-line test

Cover panels 1 to 3. Read panel 4 alone. If it's funny on its own and funnier with the setup, draw it. If it only makes sense with the setup, it's the setup.

### Off the page

The same checks run on magic patter, where the laugh has a second job: it [disarms before the astonishment](/performer-playbook#8-disarm-before-you-astonish), so the impossible moment lands as delight instead of dread. The one-beat closer, the bill coming back to me, and never making the spectator the butt of it all carry over straight. And a story told live follows the same spine as a strip, which is the [storytelling](/storytelling#telling-them-live) page.
