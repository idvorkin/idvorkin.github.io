A belief is an equation: some thing that happens equals, or causes, some meaning. "I skipped my workout, so I'm undisciplined." Mind-lines are short conversational moves that loosen that equation by pointing your attention somewhere new: down into the details, back to the intention, forward to the consequences, or up to a bigger frame. Michael Hall and Bobby Bodenhamer catalogued twenty of them, and this page runs every one against the same belief so you can see the moves side by side.

## Where mind-lines come from

Mind-lines are Hall and Bodenhamer's name for the NLP "sleight of mouth" patterns. They renamed them because "sleight of mouth" sounds like manipulation, and they wanted a label that pointed at what the patterns actually change: the lines we draw in our minds between events and meanings. My notes on Robert Dilts' version of the same toolkit are in [Sleight of Mouth](/sleight-of-mouth). Dilts lists fourteen patterns. Hall and Bodenhamer hang twenty on one formula and seven directions, which makes the whole set easier to hold in your head.

The source is [_Mind-Lines: Lines for Changing Minds_](https://www.amazon.com/s?k=Mind-Lines+Lines+for+Changing+Minds+Hall+Bodenhamer) by L. Michael Hall and Bobby G. Bodenhamer (second edition, E.T. Publications).

## The magic box: EB = IS

Nothing means anything by itself. Meaning gets made when a person links an **external behavior** (EB, something you could catch on video) to an **internal state** (IS, a feeling, judgment or significance). The book calls that link the "magic box" and writes it as EB = IS.

The link comes in two flavors:

- **Cause-effect:** "When you raise your voice, you make me anxious."
- **Complex equivalence:** "When you raise your voice, it means you don't respect me."

A belief is a thought you have said "yes" to. That "yes" sits one level above the thought, which is why beliefs feel like facts rather than opinions. Reframing starts the moment you put any other frame around the thought: a "maybe," a "since when?", or a story.

Before you can reframe anything, you need the equation. People usually say only half of it ("I'm so stressed"), so the first job is to ask for the missing side: "What does that mean to you?" for the meaning, "How do you know?" for the evidence.

## Seven directions, twenty lines

Every mind-line sends attention in one of seven directions. The book's own simplification is even shorter: there are only two levels. Lines 1–4 work **inside the box** and change the content of the equation. Lines 5–20 work **outside the box**: they leave the content alone and wrap a new frame (time, values, identity, a story) around it, and the frame changes what the content means.

| Direction         | Lines | The move                                            |
| ----------------- | ----- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Deframing         | 1–2   | Go down into the details until the belief dissolves |
| Content reframing | 3–4   | Give the behavior or the meaning a new definition   |
| Counter reframing | 5–7   | Turn the belief back on itself, or find exceptions  |
| Pre-framing       | 8–9   | Go back in time to the intention or cause           |
| Post-framing      | 10–12 | Go forward in time to the consequences              |
| Outframing        | 13–19 | Go up and wrap a bigger concept around the belief   |
| Analogous framing | 20    | Go sideways into a story                            |

My original notes listed 26 framings, which came from a later edition that adds patterns such as both/and, negation and possibility framing. The second edition this rewrite is based on has twenty, and says outright that the list isn't exhaustive: any concept you can wrap around a belief is a potential mind-line.

## All twenty on one belief

The running belief: **"Skipping my workout means I'm undisciplined."** The EB is skipping this morning's workout. The IS is "I'm undisciplined."

### Deframing: go down

| #   | Mind-line        | The move                                                         | Example                                                                                                                                     |
| --- | ---------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1   | Chunk down       | Ask for specifics until the vague belief has nothing to stand on | "Which workout? Undisciplined about what: your whole life, or 7 a.m. on one Tuesday?"                                                       |
| 2   | Reality strategy | Ask how the person builds the belief, step by step               | "How do you know you're undisciplined? Do you see the empty calendar slot first, hear a voice, or feel the sinking and then find the word?" |

### Content reframing: redefine inside the box

| #   | Mind-line      | The move                                   | Example                                                                                             |
| --- | -------------- | ------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 3   | Reframe the EB | Call the behavior something else           | "You didn't skip a workout. You took the rest day your sore shoulder was asking for."               |
| 4   | Reframe the IS | Show what would actually earn that meaning | "Undisciplined is a month with no plan and no training. One miss in a steady routine is a Tuesday." |

### Counter reframing: turn it back on itself

| #   | Mind-line                     | The move                                              | Example                                                                                             |
| --- | ----------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 5   | Apply the EB to self/listener | Apply the behavior in the belief to the belief itself | "You're skipping something too: the four workouts you did do this week."                            |
| 6   | Apply the IS to self/listener | Apply the meaning to the speaker or the listener      | "So when I miss a run, I'm undisciplined too?"                                                      |
| 7   | Counter-example               | Find the cases where the equation fails               | "Have you ever missed a session and still been the disciplined one, say, the week you had the flu?" |

### Pre-framing: go back in time

| #   | Mind-line                | The move                                                   | Example                                                                                         |
| --- | ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 8   | Positive prior intention | Name the good purpose the behavior was serving             | "You were protecting your sleep, and sleep is part of training too."                            |
| 9   | Positive prior cause     | Attribute the behavior to a cause that leaves room to grow | "You were up past midnight with a sick kid. The workout lost to something you care about more." |

### Post-framing: go forward in time

| #   | Mind-line          | The move                                 | Example                                                                                     |
| --- | ------------------ | ---------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 10  | First outcome      | Show where believing this leads next     | "If you call yourself undisciplined, tomorrow's workout becomes a test you expect to fail." |
| 11  | Outcome of outcome | Follow that consequence one step further | "And a few failed tests later, the label becomes your reason to quit the habit you built."  |
| 12  | Eternity framing   | Look back on it from the end of a life   | "At 80, will you remember this Tuesday, or decades of mostly showing up?"                   |

### Outframing: go up

| #   | Mind-line           | The move                                                | Example                                                                                               |
| --- | ------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 13  | Model of the world  | Show the belief is one map, made by someone             | "Who taught you that one miss is a character flaw? That's a drill sergeant's map, not the territory." |
| 14  | Criteria and values | Bring a higher value to bear on the belief              | "What matters more: an unbroken streak, or still being strong at 70?"                                 |
| 15  | Allness             | Apply it to everyone, always, and see if it still holds | "So every athlete who has ever taken an unplanned day off is undisciplined?"                          |
| 16  | Have-to             | Question the necessity baked into the belief            | "Do you have to hit every scheduled session to count as disciplined? What happens if you don't?"      |
| 17  | Identity            | Separate the act from the person                        | "You're a person who skipped a workout, not an undisciplined person."                                 |
| 18  | Other abstractions  | Name the belief as an instance of some broader concept  | "This is all-or-nothing scoring, isn't it? A streak counter isn't a character test."                  |
| 19  | Ecology             | Ask whether the belief serves you                       | "Does this belief get you to the gym more often, or just make rest days miserable?"                   |

### Analogous framing: go sideways

| #   | Mind-line          | The move                                              | Example                                                                                                               |
| --- | ------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 20  | Metaphor and story | Tell a story with the same shape and a better meaning | "A plane is off course most of the flight. It lands because the pilot keeps correcting, not because it never drifts." |

## Using them well

- **Get the equation first.** You can't reframe "I'm stressed" until you know what the stress is about and what it means.
- **Start by deframing.** The authors moved chunking down to the front of the second edition because it worked best in their workshops. A vague belief often falls apart once someone asks "specifically?"
- **Stack them.** One line rarely does it. Several in a row, from different directions, land harder than any single one.
- **Not every line fits every belief.** Keep trying directions and watch the other person's face; the shift shows up there before it shows up in words.
- **Keep the outcome kind.** These patterns can just as easily make you a smart-ass who wins arguments and loses friends. Used to pry someone's belief open against their interest, they are manipulation. Used to give a person, or yourself, a more useful frame, they are a gift.

The easiest place to practice is on your own inner critic. It speaks in tidy EB = IS equations, and running a few mind-lines against one is a fast way to find out whether it's telling the truth or just being loud.
