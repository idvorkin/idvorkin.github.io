My back goes out a few times a year. My mood tracks the Meta stock price more than I'd like to admit. Twenty years ago I'd meet those moments with a thrash cycle — fused with the pain, angry at my body, tangled up in a story about why this was unfair — which made the experience much worse than the sensation itself. Now I aspire to [be calm like water](/affirmations), be present, remember that _this too shall pass_, and work the problem. I still catch myself mid-thrash often enough to know it's aspiration, not habit.

The move I'm aiming at has many names. [7 Habits](/be-proactive) calls it _the space between stimulus and response_ — the gap Viktor Frankl said is where our freedom lives. Buddhism calls it _equanimity_. ACT therapy calls it _psychological flexibility_. Same move, named by three different traditions, arrived at from three different starting premises.

These are my working notes on the convergence — reverse-engineered Buddhism. The thesis: **pain is the signal. Suffering is what I add. Equanimity is the capacity to stop adding.** Buddhism built a 2500-year practice around that capacity. ACT is the behavioral-science packaging that turns it into a repeatable skill instead of a lucky mood. Practical, repeatable under stress, and (mostly) resistant to my own excuses.

## What ACT is

ACT — Acceptance and Commitment Therapy — is CBT's younger sibling, built by Steven Hayes and colleagues in the 1980s. CBT asks whether a thought is true. ACT asks whether fighting it works. Usually it doesn't, so ACT stops arguing with the thought and spends the energy on action instead.

The name is the whole program. _Acceptance_: make room for the feeling instead of fighting it. Not liking it — just not spending energy trying to get rid of it. _Commitment_: do the thing that matters anyway. Values drive the bus, not feelings.

Underneath is a claim Buddhism made 2500 years ago: most suffering is the _second arrow_, the layer I add by pushing away what I don't want (aversion) and clutching what I do (grasping). I can't always dodge the first arrow. The second one is almost always optional.

At the bottom: the hard situation and whatever it drags in — the thought, the feeling, the sensation. Two arrows out of it. _Away moves_ are what I do when I'm hooked, the things I want less of. _Towards moves_ are what I do when I'm unhooked, the things I want more of. The claim that sounds too big until you try it: almost every psychological problem — anxiety, depression, addiction, you name it — reduces to the same loop. Hooked, then an away move. The work is noticing the hook and switching arrows.

Three nuances that took me a minute to get:

1. **Behavior includes the stuff inside my head.** Rumination is a covert away move; unhooking is a covert towards move. If a camera could record it, it's overt; if not, it's covert. Both kinds belong on the diagram.
2. **Any activity can be either, depending on function.** Gorging chocolate to numb out a feeling I don't want to sit with is an away move. Savoring a square of good chocolate with Tori after dinner is a towards move. Never the form, always the function. (Sometimes it takes me half the bar to notice.)
3. **I define what's an away move, not someone else.** If I call a behavior a towards move and it turns out to be an away move, I'll find out later. The diagram is a snapshot of my own worldview, and the work surfaces the truth on its own timeline.

The dot where the arrows split is a seam I've written about before. Covey calls it [the space between stimulus and response](/be-proactive) — _respond, don't react_. Atomic Habits calls it [the cue](/habits), and gives me the engineering layer for shaping the response. Same seam; ACT is what you do at it. And every move is a tiny vote for the person I'm becoming, which is the whole argument of [/mortality-software](/mortality-software).

{% include summarize-page.html src="/be-proactive" %}

Which arrow I take turns on one question, and it is never "is this thought true?" ACT doesn't debate the content of thoughts. It asks:

> If I let this thought guide my behavior, will that help me create a richer, fuller, more meaningful life?

That's the whole therapy in one question. Harris calls it _workability_. There's a client transcript where someone insists "I really am fat, look at me," and the therapist refuses to argue: in this room we never debate whether a thought is true or false, only whether it helps you live a better life. The refusal to engage _is_ the treatment.

It works because the same thought does different things in different contexts. Fused with "I'm not good enough," I cancel my plans. Unhooked, the same thought is noticed, acknowledged, and I go anyway. Nothing about the content changed; only the grip. So when a thought is bothering me I try to skip true-or-false entirely and ask: _is living as if this were true taking me toward the person I want to be?_ It's faster than arguing with my own mind, and it's the question underneath every good [coaching](/coaching) question I've ever been asked.

## Why I get stuck: hooked and running

Two things go wrong, and they feed each other.

**Getting hooked** (the clinical word is _cognitive fusion_) means a thought is running my behavior. I'm not observing "I'm not good enough"; I'm wearing it, and I cancel on a friend. The hooks come in six flavors, and naming which one I'm on makes the unhooking move easier to pick: the past (rumination, regret), the future (worry, catastrophizing), self-concept ("I'm broken," "I'm always right"), reasons (all the reasons I can't), rules (should, must, fair, unfair) and judgments (of anything). A typical story braids three of them: because bad things happened to me (past), I am damaged (self-concept), so I can't do X (reason). I've been fighting these six under other names for a decade — see [/mind-monsters](/mind-monsters).

**Running** (_experiential avoidance_) means spending energy trying to get rid of a feeling I don't want. A glass of wine to kill the anxiety. Scrolling to drown out the boredom. Skipping the hard conversation because I don't want the guilt. Small doses are fine — this isn't purism. The problem is when the escape hatch becomes the life, which is my one-line definition of [addiction](/addiction).

The two link up: I fuse with "this feeling is unbearable," and that drives the running. Thoughts and feelings are not the problem. Getting hooked by them is.

Before anything new, the audit. Harris calls it _creative hopelessness_ — not hopelessness about life, hopelessness about the agenda of controlling how I feel. Inventory everything I've tried to make an unwanted feeling go away. The acronym is **DOTS**: **D**istraction (TV, scrolling, food), **O**pting out (avoiding the people and places that trigger it), **T**hinking strategies (positive thinking, arguing with the thought, suppressing, ruminating) and **S**ubstances (alcohol, drugs, even exercise when it's an escape). Then two questions per item: how has it worked in the long term, and what has it cost? The honest frame is that most of it works in the short term. Distraction does distract. Avoiding the phone call does kill the guilt for an afternoon. None of it works long-term, and the costs compound.

My DOTS: TikTok for boredom, stalling on hard conversations, over-checking work to silence "not good enough," exercise to burn off anxiety. None of it bad in small doses; all of it a problem as the go-to move instead of feeling the feeling and acting anyway. My running tally of exactly this trade — short-term relief against long-term cost — is [/anxiety-management](/anxiety-management).

## Six moves, one picture

The whole program has one aim. Harris calls it psychological flexibility; I call it **equanimity**: a stable inner ground from which I can act on what matters, right now, guided by who I want to be. (I'm using the word in the expansive contemporary sense — steady inside _and_ acting outside. The strict classical sense is narrower; see the map at the end.) The more equanimous I am, the better I respond to whatever shows up, and the more _vitality_ I get out of living. Vitality is not a feeling. My word for it is sustained motivation: fully alive and engaged with what's in front of me, whether or not the inside is pleasant. I can feel vital in the middle of grief, with my back screaming at me, some days with my gut doing whatever PSC is doing. Vitality ≠ feeling good. That line alone was worth the price of the book.

My younger self read "act on values regardless of feelings" and went full robot — suppress the feeling, be rational, override with will. It half-worked. I got good at forcing action, but the feelings didn't go away; they piled up in the basement until something broke. ACT's move is the opposite order: let the feeling be loud, accept that it's here, act on the value anyway. Equanimity over robot-mode, every time. My "Calm like water" [affirmation](/affirmations) is pointing at exactly this — water has equanimity, a mountain has rigidity.

{% include summarize-page.html src="/affirmations" %}

Six moves get me there. Harris draws them as a hexagon and calls it the _hexaflex_. They compress to three words — **Open, Present, Do What Matters** — which is the shortest diagnostic I've found when I'm stuck, and I'm stuck a lot: _am I open? am I present? am I doing what matters?_ They're not a sequence. Stuck on one, move to another.

### 1. Defusion: unhook from the thought

Step back and watch my thinking instead of tangling with it. Thoughts are words and pictures, nothing more or less; hold them lightly, let them guide me but not run me.

The metaphor is _Hands as Thoughts_. Hands over my face — that's fusion. I can barely see the room, can't engage with anyone, can't do anything useful. Lower them to my lap. The thoughts haven't gone anywhere; now I can see, move and act.

The practice: take "I'm a loser" and replay it as "I'm having the thought that I'm a loser," then "I notice I'm having the thought that I'm a loser." Half the sting, instantly. The point is never to make the thought go away — that's avoidance dressed up in mindfulness clothes. The point is to do what matters _while the thought is still there_.

My [anxiety protocol](/anxiety) is a live-fire defusion drill: notice the gap between reality and expectation, unhook from the catastrophizing, act from values instead.

### 2. Acceptance: make room for the feeling

Also called willingness, expansion, opening up. Let the unwanted feeling be there. Not liking it, not wanting it — allowing it. This is never passive acceptance of my situation: change what I can, and make room for the pain that shows up while I do. It's Acceptance _and_ Commitment for a reason.

The metaphor is the _Struggle Switch_. Switch on: I must get rid of this bad feeling, so now I have anxiety about my anxiety, anger about my anger, a feedback loop that amplifies whatever I started with. Switch off: the feeling is still unpleasant, but I'm not pouring fuel on it. It's a 0-to-10 dial, not a toggle — moving from 9 to 3 is a big win.

The practice is the three As: _acknowledge_ the feeling, _allow_ it to be there, _accommodate_ it — offer the unwanted houseguest a chair. Acceptance isn't tolerance. Tolerance is gritted teeth. Acceptance is "I don't like this, and I'm letting it be here anyway."

De Mello's _waking up from the sleep of unconscious living_ ([/awareness](/awareness)) is the same move one layer up: I can't accept what I haven't noticed, and noticing without the struggle switch on is exactly the posture.

### 3. Present: notice X

Also called _flexible attention_ — narrow, broaden, sustain or redirect my focus as the situation demands. Inflexible attention costs me three ways: cutting off (missing the person I'm with), missing out (dinner without tasting it), and doing things poorly (mistakes because I'm in my head). [/mind-at-work](/mind-at-work) is what that costs at a desk.

ACT itself avoids the word "mindfulness." The word is broken — monks, incense, long silent sits — and the skill underneath is three words: **notice X**. Breath, feet on the floor, sounds in the room, the taste of coffee, the feeling of fear. Meditation is one of hundreds of ways to train it; the framework does not require formal practice. That reframe is why I stopped bouncing off it. Every time I'd bounced before, it was because I believed mindfulness meant sitting still with my eyes closed. I can notice X on a run along Hood Canal. [Search Inside Yourself](/siy) is this skill in engineer vocabulary.

The practice is _Notice Your Hand_: five minutes of a curious child's attention on my own hand. Time evaporates. Then the follow-up: what would happen if I paid this kind of attention to Tori? To the kids?

### 4. Kernel mode: notice that I'm noticing

Your brain runs in two modes. User space is the apps: thoughts, feelings, the stories about who I am. **Kernel mode** is the layer underneath that sees all of it and isn't any of it. Every "notice X" runs there — there is always a noticer doing the noticing.

ACT calls this _self-as-context_ and piles up synonyms (observing self, noticing self, pure awareness, the continuous you) because none of them land. Kernel mode works for me because it's honest that it's a layer, not a thing. Harris's version: I am the sky, thoughts and feelings are the weather, and weather cannot harm the sky.

The practice is cheap. Inside anything I'm already doing — breath, feet on the floor, dropping anchor — add one line: _and there's a part of me noticing this_. No metaphor delivers the experience; that sentence, repeated, does. My "Calm like water" affirmation points at the same place — water is the medium, not any particular wave — and it's what I've been circling as _the awareness underneath the content_ on [/awareness](/awareness) for years. The full ladder of exercises, from the one-second seed to the fifteen-minute Continuous You, is in [Chapter 25](/act-made-simple#chapter-25-the-noticing-self).

### 5. Values: directions, not goals

Values are desired qualities of ongoing action — how I want to behave, right now and on an ongoing basis. Not goals. Getting married is a goal; once I'm married, it's done. Being loving is a value; I can act on it or neglect it in every moment for the rest of my life. Even paralyzed from the waist down, even in a refugee camp, I can still be kind, curious, honest. A goal can be stolen from me. A value cannot.

The metaphor is _Two Kids in the Car_: two kids going to Disneyland, one whining "are we there yet?", the other playing I-spy. Same goal, radically different journey.

The practice is _The Video of Your Mistakenly Held Funeral_: imagine you were presumed dead, a funeral was held, and later you get to watch the video. What would you _love_ to hear the people you love saying about the kind of person you were? I prefer this to the standard eulogy exercise because the question is what I'd love to hear, not what they'd say. [My own eulogy](/eulogy) is my working answer, and my [affirmations](/affirmations) are the same list in a different format. One rule from Harris's six: hold values lightly. Fused with, they become rigid rules. Carry them like a compass in a backpack, not clutched in a fist.

### 6. Committed action: do the thing

Translate values into ongoing patterns of behavior. Goal setting, planning, exposure — all of it rides on values, not on _should_. Without values underneath, a goal is another should, and it fails on schedule.

The test is the _dead person's goal_: if a corpse can achieve your goal better than you can, it's not a goal. "I'll stop yelling at the kids" — the corpse never yells. "When the kids push my buttons, I'll drop anchor and speak calmly" — a live person's goal. Any "I won't X" rewrites to "I will Y instead." And for any stuck situation, the _Challenge Formula_: leave; stay and live my values and make room for the pain; or stay and give up. Three is the default. Two is the work.

The practice, when I'm not doing the thing, is **HARD**: am I **H**ooked on a thought, **A**voiding discomfort, **R**emote from why it matters, or chasing a **D**oubtful goal? I can usually name it in under a minute, and it's usually D — I set the goal too big and don't start. Shrink it until I'd bet 7 out of 10 that I'll do it. [/goals](/goals) is where I audit committed action against my values each year, and [/y26](/y26) is this year's plan — this move, in this format.

## Which move when

The framework is non-linear — I can pick up at any process at any time — but that's hard to act on without a crude decision tree. Here's mine:

- **I'm stuck in a thought loop (rumination, worry, self-criticism)** → defusion. Notice, name, neutralize. "I'm having the thought that..."
- **I'm overwhelmed or dissociating** → drop anchor. ACE: acknowledge, come back, engage.
- **I'm avoiding something that matters because it would hurt** → acceptance. Make room for the feeling so I can take the action.
- **I'm drifting, disengaged, going through the motions** → contact with the present moment. Notice X with curiosity.
- **I'm fused with a story about who I am ("I'm broken," "I'm a loser")** → self-as-context. Step back to kernel mode. The thought is a leaf, not the river.
- **I have no direction, everything feels pointless** → values. Mistakenly-held funeral, or "ten years from now, looking back."
- **I know what to do but can't get myself to do it** → committed action plus HARD diagnostic.

## Two allies: kindness and the feeling itself

Once the feeling is allowed in, there are two things to do with it.

Kindness first. Compassion in six words: **acknowledge pain and respond with kindness**. Self-compassion is the same move turned inward, and it's a behavior, not a mood — I can do it whether I feel it or not. The opener is the _Two Friends_ question. When I'm suffering, do I want the friend who says "stop whining, suck it up," or the friend who says "this is rough, I'm here for you, we're in this together"? Then the uncomfortable follow-up: _which friend am I being to myself?_ The practice is _Kind Hand_: when a feeling has a physical location — tight chest, churning gut, lump in the throat — put a hand on that spot and imagine warmth flowing from it. Not to dissolve the feeling. To hold it. My ["Class Act"](/class-act) affirmation is the voice I'm talking to myself with when I get this right, and [/curious](/curious) is my longer take on compassion — aimed at others first, then turned inward.

Then the feeling itself. Emotions aren't the problem; they're messengers, and each one evolved to do three jobs: _communicate_ ("I'm in danger," "I need help"), _motivate_ (fight, flee, rest, bond) and _illuminate_ what I care about. The size of the feeling is the size of the gap between what I want and what I have. Sadness says I loved something. Anger says a line I care about got crossed. Fear says something I value is at risk. Once I've accepted the feeling instead of fighting it, I can ask it: _what are you telling me about what matters to me?_ The answer is almost always obvious in hindsight, and I usually missed it because I was busy trying to make the feeling go away. Harris's slogan: your pain is your ally. The work isn't to eliminate hard emotions. It's to stop cutting the communication cable. This framing changed how I talk to myself about feelings; the running notes are at [/emotions](/emotions).

## Monday

Five things, each keyed to the moment I'd need it.

1. _When I'm hooked:_ "I'm having the thought that I'm a loser." Then: "I notice I'm having the thought that I'm a loser." Half the sting, instantly.
2. _When the storm hits:_ ACE. Acknowledge what's inside. Come back into my body — feet into the floor, spine straight. Engage with the world — five things I see, three I hear. The anchor doesn't stop the storm. It holds me still.
3. _When I don't know what to do:_ the one question. If I let this thought run me, does it take me toward the person I want to be? Not "is it true." Never "is it true."
4. _When I'm not doing the thing:_ HARD. Hooked? Avoiding the discomfort? Remote from why it matters? Doubtful goal? It's usually D for me. Shrink the goal until I'd bet 7 out of 10 that I'll do it.
5. _Sunday night:_ towards or away? Five minutes. What moved me toward the life I want this week, what moved me away. Data, not judgment.

The rest of the toolbox — Leaves on a Stream, Kind Hand, the Continuous You, the Challenge Formula — lives in the move sections above and in the [chapter notes](/act-made-simple). These five are the ones I want in muscle memory.

## The map back

For the reader who wants the clinical and Buddhist vocabulary. All six moves have direct Buddhist ancestors — ACT's founders were explicit that they were rebuilding contemplative practice in the language of behavioral science. ACT strips out the cosmology, the rebirth talk and the monastic container, and keeps the practical moves. That's its strength (accessible to anyone) and its weakness (reinvents wheels that Buddhism already labeled).

<details markdown="1">
<summary>The four-column map: my words, Harris's words, academic ACT, Buddhist concept</summary>

| My words                                             | Harris's words                         | Academic ACT                           | Buddhist concept                                      |
| ---------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| equanimity — stable ground, acting on what matters   | psychological flexibility              | psychological flexibility              | equanimity (_upekkhā_)                                |
| getting hooked                                       | getting hooked                         | cognitive fusion                       | identification with thought                           |
| unhooking, stepping back                             | unhooking                              | defusion                               | non-attachment / noting practice                      |
| running from feelings                                | the struggle switch                    | experiential avoidance                 | aversion (_dvesha_) — pushing away, the second arrow  |
| chasing good feelings                                | the happiness trap                     | experiential control (pleasure agenda) | grasping (_tanha_) — craving, clinging, "more please" |
| kernel mode — the layer underneath that just watches | the observing self                     | self-as-context                        | _anatta_ (non-self) / witness awareness               |
| presence practice                                    | dropping anchor                        | contact with the present moment        | _sati_ (mindfulness)                                  |
| does it work? (not: is it true?)                     | workability                            | workability                            | skillful vs. unskillful action                        |
| toward moves / away moves                            | toward / away moves (the choice point) | committed action vs. avoidance         | right intention / right action                        |

The Buddhist column isn't claiming perfect 1:1 equivalence — 2500 years of refinement against a 40-year synthesis means some rows are tighter than others — but the parallels are too strong to ignore. One honesty note on the top row: I'm using _equanimity_ in the expansive contemporary sense (Thich Nhat Hanh, Kornfield, engaged Buddhism), broad enough to cover the integrated "acting on values from stable ground" move that ACT calls psychological flexibility. Strict classical equanimity (_upekkhā_ as one of the Four Immeasurables) is narrower — inner non-reactivity only, without the values-and-action overlay. Scholarly comparisons of ACT to Buddhism (e.g., Fung 2015) map psychological flexibility onto the Noble Eightfold Path as an integrated path. I find "equanimity" gets the feel right for a lay reader; the Eightfold Path framing is there if you want to go deeper.
