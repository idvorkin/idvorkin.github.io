Loneliness isn't being alone. It's the feeling of missing a connection you need, and the danger isn't that it hurts. It's that it barely hurts. Hunger gets louder until you eat; loneliness mostly doesn't, so we drift into isolation without the alarm ever going off. Adam Mastroianni [calls this](https://www.experimental-history.com/p/there-is-no-epidemic-of-loneliness) social scurvy: sailors short on vitamin C withered without ever craving an orange. Friendship used to happen to us anyway. School, work, church, and the neighborhood put the same people in front of us week after week, and friends fell out of that for free. Those machines are mostly gone, so I can't wait until I feel lonely. I have to build the machine myself: the same place, the same people, often enough, for long enough.

## What loneliness is

**Connection** is a relationship where a change in one party affects the other. **Loneliness** is the felt absence of that connection. It's not about how many people are around you, but whether the interactions you have carry any weight.

That makes it different from solitude. **Solitude** is chosen, and often where my best thinking and calm come from. **Loneliness** is unwanted. You can be lonely in a crowded room, and content alone on a mountain.

It does hurt some. The brain treats social pain a lot like physical pain, which is why a snub stings in the body and not just in the head. But the craving for company is weak. When researchers cut people off from food or from company for ten hours, both cravings rose, but hunger rose much faster, and 10% of the isolated group ended the day craving company no more than when they started ([Tomova et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-020-00742-z)). Our ancestors never needed a strong craving for people, because they couldn't survive without them, so evolution never built one. You can be starving for people without feeling hungry for them, and the bill comes anyway: chronic loneliness is linked to early death, depression, anxiety, heart disease, substance abuse, and domestic abuse ([Loneliness in America, Harvard MCC](https://mcc.gse.harvard.edu/reports/loneliness-in-america)).

**Summary definition**: Loneliness is the felt lack of meaningful connection, and the feeling runs far quieter than the lack.

## The friendship recession

Careful with the word "epidemic." In a 2021 Harvard survey, 36% of Americans said they felt lonely frequently or almost all the time, including 61% of 18-25 year olds and 51% of mothers with young children ([Loneliness in America, Harvard MCC](https://mcc.gse.harvard.edu/reports/loneliness-in-america)). But Mastroianni's [read of the data](https://www.experimental-history.com/p/there-is-no-epidemic-of-loneliness) is that _feeling_ lonely isn't clearly rising. An age gap like that is a snapshot, not a trend; when you compare today's young people to young people decades ago, the changes are small and mixed. What has collapsed is time with other people. We're not much lonelier, we're more alone, and because it barely hurts, nobody treats it as an emergency. From the [Survey Center on American Life's 2021 friendship report](https://www.americansurveycenter.org/research/the-state-of-american-friendship-change-challenges-and-loss/):

- Americans with **no close friends**: 3% in 1990, 12% in 2021.
- Americans with **ten or more close friends**: 33% in 1990, 13% in 2021.
- 39% have **online-only friendships**; only 8% have ever made a close friend online.

Time with friends is falling too. The American Time Use Survey shows the average American spent about 6.5 hours a week with friends in 2010-2013, just over 4 hours by 2019, and under 3 hours in 2021 ([Scripps, on Bryce Ward's analysis](https://www.scrippsnews.com/life/why-are-americans-spending-more-time-alone)). Teenagers are ahead of the curve: today's 12th graders spend an hour less a day with friends in person than Gen X did at the same age ([Twenge](https://theconversation.com/teens-have-less-face-time-with-their-friends-and-are-lonelier-than-ever-113240)).

Why this matters more than it sounds:

- **Weak social ties are a mortality risk on par with smoking.** A meta-analysis of 148 studies found people with stronger relationships had a 50% higher likelihood of survival over the study periods ([Holt-Lunstad et al., PLoS Medicine](https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article?id=10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316)).
- **Doubling your real-life friends boosts well-being about as much as a 50% raise.** The size of your online network, by contrast, is uncorrelated with well-being ([NBER](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18690)). I wonder if there's someone in Meta working on changing this.

### Men have it worst

Once married, many men lean on their wife as their only real friend: 85% of married men turn to their spouse first with a personal problem, versus 72% of married women ([same report](https://www.americansurveycenter.org/research/the-state-of-american-friendship-change-challenges-and-loss/)). SNL's [Man Park](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=9XOt2Vh0T8w) nails it: a dog park where wives drop off their husbands so the guys can finally talk to each other.

## Why it happens

Nobody decided to have fewer friends. Friendship has requirements, and modern life quietly stopped paying for them.

**Friendship has four ingredients.** Research on how friendships form keeps finding the same things: you have to **see each other regularly** (nearly all friendships start at school, work, or in the neighborhood), you have to **put in hours** (more below), it helps to be at the **same life stage**, and it helps to **share an interest** with real enthusiasm behind it. None of these is optional, and all of them are expensive.

**The hours are brutal.** Jeffrey Hall's research puts it at roughly 40-60 hours together to become casual friends, 80-100 to become friends, and 200+ to become close friends. Hours at work count for less than hours spent hanging out ([University of Kansas](https://news.ku.edu/news/article/2018/03/06/study-reveals-number-hours-it-takes-make-friend)). Compare that to the 4 hours a week the average adult now spends with all their friends combined.

**The defaults that paid for those hours are gone.** School handed you proximity and repetition for free. Then work and family take over the calendar, and the community institutions that used to fill the gap (clubs, volunteering, [organized religion](/religion)) have thinned out. Nothing replaced them. Digital contact feels like it should, but online-only friends don't show up in the well-being data.

**Life keeps reshuffling the deck.** Moving cities, going remote, having kids, and retiring each quietly drop a set of friends and don't automatically supply new ones. Every [life chapter](/chapters) means starting the clock over on somebody.

**Convenience is a ratchet.** Staying in is always easier than going out, every night in makes the next one easier, and the fewer people you see, the more awkward it feels to reach back out. The alarm that should push back stays quiet, so isolation feeds itself.

## What actually works

### Happiness is contagious, but only up close

A 20-year study of the Framingham social network found that happiness spreads up to three degrees of separation: to your friends, their friends, and their friends ([Fowler & Christakis, BMJ](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2600606/)). The details are what make it useful:

- A friend who lives within a mile becoming happy raises your odds of being happy by 25%. A next-door neighbor: 34%. A nearby sibling: 14%. A co-resident spouse: 8%.
- Coworkers: no measurable effect.
- The effect decays with both distance and time. Recent, close, physical contact is what counts.
- People central to their network, not just connected to it, are more likely to be happy later.

So the levers are physical proximity and frequency. That matches both the four ingredients above and the Harvard Study of Adult Development, which after more than 75 years of following the same families found the quality of your relationships to be the best predictor of health and happiness in old age. Robert Waldinger and Marc Schulz wrote it up as _The Good Life_, and the phrase of theirs I keep is **social fitness**: relationships are like muscles, and they atrophy without regular work.

### The practices

1. **Don't wait to feel lonely.** The alarm is too quiet to steer by. Treat it like thirst, which is also unreliable, so you drink on a schedule. Mastroianni's prescription is the same brute force: "you must sometimes hang out even when you’re not lonely." Everything below is a way to do that.
2. **Pick a place and keep showing up.** A weekly class, a gym, a club, a standing game night. The point is the same faces on a schedule, not the activity. This is proximity and repetition, bought on purpose instead of inherited from school. It has to be in person, and it has to include people outside your house: as Mastroianni puts it, "face time, not just FaceTime."
3. **Bank the hours.** If a close friend costs 200 hours, a quarterly dinner will never get there. Consistency beats intensity: a regular small thing outbuilds an occasional big one.
4. **Put friends on the calendar like workouts.** Social fitness means treating friendship as maintenance, not a reward for having free time. Attention is the investment; schedule it or it won't happen.
5. **Lean into the awkward.** New friendships require some discomfort, and novel shared experiences (a trip, a project, a hard thing done together) build them faster than another coffee. It goes better than you expect: people reliably predict that talking to a stranger will go worse than it actually does ([Sandstrom & Boothby, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1080/15298868.2020.1816568)).
6. **Find people at your life stage.** Shared challenges make natural bonds. The new parents at the park, the other people who just moved, the other person whose kids just left.
7. **Make the small reach-out a daily habit.** The text, the call, the invite. Each one is tiny; the sum is the ratchet running in reverse.
8. **It's never too late.** That's the title of the last chapter of _The Good Life_, and the authors mean it literally. The 200 hours start whenever you start.
