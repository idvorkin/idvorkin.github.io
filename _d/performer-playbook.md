For years I've called myself the Dealer of Smiles. My [eulogy](/eulogy) says Igor lived to see eyes go wide and mouths gape with wonder, and I mean it — the magic, the juggling, the balloons, the closet full of ridiculous shirts are all just tools for one job: handing a stranger a moment they didn't see coming. This post is the how. After enough shows I noticed I keep running the same nine rules in my head, whether I'm doing one card trick for one kid or working a whole room, so I finally wrote them down.

## The Nine Rules

### 1. Aim for the impossible moment, not the trick

A trick is a sequence of moves. The effect is the one instant where their model of reality breaks. I plan backwards from that beat and cut everything that doesn't feed it. When someone gasps, that sound is their brain rewriting what's possible on the fly — that's the whole product, and everything else is just delivery.

### 2. Perform for the group, not the person

Even when the card is in one person's hand, I'm working the circle around them. What I'm really after is the moment they stop looking at me and look at each other — that glance is the wonder doubling. A gasp alone is nice; a gasp that jumps around a group is the thing they remember a year later.

### 3. Give the moment room

My instinct when I'm nervous is to fill the gap and get to the next thing. Wrong move. After the reveal I shut up and let the silence sit. That pause is where the feeling actually lands — step on it and there's nothing left to keep.

### 4. Make them the hero

The stronger version of every effect happens in the spectator's own hands, not mine. Their card, their signature on it, their free choice — so the impossible thing happened to _them_, not to me showing off in front of them. Nobody goes home bragging about how skilled I am. They go home telling people what happened to _them_.

### 5. Let your delight cue theirs

If I'm delighted, everyone else gets permission to be delighted too. [Joy is contagious](/joy) — the room takes its read from me, so I have to actually feel it, not fake it. Robert-Houdin said a magician is an actor playing the part of a magician; I'm playing the part of a guy who can't believe this is happening either. I'm serving the feeling in the room, not fishing for applause.

### 6. Engineer a peak and a good ending

People don't remember the average of an experience. They remember the best moment and the last moment. So I design one unmistakable peak and I never let the set dribble out — land the finish clean, on the strongest beat I have, and quit while they still want more.

### 7. Give them a story simple enough to retell

The real test isn't the reaction in the moment. It's whether they can retell it at dinner tomorrow. If the story needs five conditions and a diagram, it dies on the walk home. "He signed my card and then it was inside a lemon" survives. Enjoyment is pleasure plus people plus memory, and memory only sticks to things simple enough to say out loud.

### 8. Disarm before you astonish

Nobody feels wonder while they're braced to be embarrassed. Before I try to amaze anyone I get a laugh and make it obvious they're safe — I am never going to make them the butt of the joke. [Safety is the precondition for joy](/joy). Skip it and the exact same astonishment that should have felt like delight lands as dread instead.

### 9. Small self, big generosity

Awe has a way of shrinking you, in a good way. It quiets the ego and opens people up, and that goes for me on the working side too. When I remember I'm handing someone a moment instead of proving how clever I am, everything gets better — the pacing, the warmth, their reaction. I perform best from generosity, not from wanting to look impressive.

## The Science I'm Leaning On

None of this is my invention. I reverse-engineered it from shows that worked, then went looking for the research that explained why they did.

- **Awe = vastness + accommodation.** Dacher Keltner and Jonathan Haidt describe awe as running into something vast enough that your current picture of the world can't absorb it, so your brain has to update on the spot. That "can't file it" feeling is exactly the impossible moment in rule one.
- **Enjoyment = pleasure + people + memory.** Arthur Brooks separates cheap pleasure from durable enjoyment: enjoyment is pleasure that happened _with other people_ and got laid down as a _memory_. That's rules two, six, and seven in one line — do it together, give it a peak, make it retellable.
- **The peak-end rule.** Daniel Kahneman found we judge an experience by its peak and its ending, not its average. So I stopped trying to make every second great and started building one great second and a clean finish.
